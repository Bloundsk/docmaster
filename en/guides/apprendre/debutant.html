🎓 Learning how to learn — Beginner

What makes things stick. No prior knowledge needed.

🕒 Last updated: 16 August 2026

⚡ Key points

  • Forgetting is brutal for the first two days, then it slows down: review early.
  • Six well-placed reviews cost four times less time than weekly rereading.
  • Testing yourself is not a check on learning, it is the way of learning.
  • A text read four times becomes familiar, not known — and the difference only shows a week later.
  • All these methods have one thing in common: they are unpleasant at the time.

Introduction

This guide is about the one skill that serves all the others. The other paths on this site teach a subject; this one teaches how to make what you learn there stay.

There is a good reason to start here: the methods almost everyone uses — rereading, highlighting, recopying notes — are among the least effective there are. These are not lazy habits: they are habits that produce a feeling of progress, which is far worse.

Warning

This guide is educational. The figures quoted come from published work in the psychology of memory, and are given as orders of magnitude: they vary with the material learned, with people, and with test conditions.

Persistent learning difficulties may stem from a specific disorder, which no revision method will correct. This guide is no substitute for professional advice.

What makes things stick

📉 The forgetting curve

What you have just learned does not fade at a steady rate. The loss is brutal at first, then slower and slower. It is this shape, not the average speed, that dictates when to review.

The shape matters more than the numbers

A steady decline would mean reviewing at constant intervals. That is not what happens: most of what is going to disappear disappears in the first two days. Past that point, what remains lasts far longer.

Hence a counter-intuitive consequence: a review done the next day saves more material than one done three weeks later, for the same time spent.

Worked example — 40 terms learned today

With no review at all, 24 remain the next day, 18 after two days, 8 after a week and 3 after a month.

In other words: more than half of the total loss happens in the first 48 hours. Waiting for the weekend to review is arriving after the battle.

Why cramming works — and why it is a trap

Revising intensively the night before works very well for the next day. The curve starts high, and the exam falls before it comes down.

The problem is that it then comes down exactly the same. Three weeks later almost nothing is left — which explains the familiar feeling of having "forgotten everything" about a subject you nonetheless passed.

Common mistake — believing you learned it badly

Forgetting is not a failure of learning, it is how memory normally works. Retaining without effort something seen only once happens to nobody.

The useful question is therefore not "how do I learn better the first time", but "when should I come back to it".

💡 Forgetting is fastest just after learning. That is where a review costs least and pays most.

🔁 Spaced repetition

Each successful review does more than lift the curve back up: it flattens it. What lasted a day then lasts three days, then a week, then three. This is what makes lasting retention possible for a surprisingly small amount of work.

The intervals

A classic schedule: 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 21 days, 60 days, 180 days. Each successful review pushes the next one about three times further out.

Nothing sacred about these numbers: what matters is the principle — come back just before forgetting. Coming back too early wastes time on what is still known; coming back too late amounts to relearning.

Worked example — 60 terms to hold for six months

At 8 seconds per term per pass.

Spaced: 6 passes, that is 48 minutes in total over the six months.

Rereading everything weekly: 26 passes, that is 3 h 20.

Four times less time — and better retention at the end, because spacing works where rereading repeats.

What makes the method hard to keep up

It does not ask for effort, it asks for a schedule. That is where everything is decided: nobody keeps in their head that one term is due in 21 days and another in 3.

Hence flashcard software — Anki being the best known — which does nothing but keep that schedule. A card box and five dividers do the same job, without a screen.

Common mistake — reviewing what you already know

It is pleasant, it gives a feeling of competence, and it serves no purpose. Time spent on a card you have got right for six months is time taken from the ones you get wrong.

A good session is one where you make mistakes regularly. If everything comes out right, the intervals are too short.

💡 Spacing does not ask for more work: it asks for four times less. What it asks for is regularity.

✍️ Testing yourself rather than rereading

Searching your memory for an answer strengthens the trace far more than rereading it. This is one of the most solid findings in the field, and one of the least applied: the test is not a check on learning, it is the learning.

The reference experiment

Two groups, one text, identical study time. The first rereads; the second tests itself without looking. Tested immediately, the rereaders do better — hence the illusion.

Tested a week later, the ratio clearly reverses: about 40% retained for the rereaders, about 61% for those who had questioned themselves.

Worked example — 100 items to remember

Rereading only: 40 items still known a week later.

Half the time self-testing: 51 items.

All the time self-testing: 61 items, that is 21 more — for the same number of minutes.

Self-testing, in practice

  • Close the course and write down everything you remember. Then reopen it and see what was missing — those are exactly the points to work on.
  • Turn headings into questions. "The forgetting curve" becomes "what shape does the forgetting curve have, and what does that change?".
  • Answer before reading the answer, even if you get it wrong. Getting it wrong then seeing the correction sticks better than reading the right answer straight away.
  • Explain it to somebody, or out loud to nobody. The gaps show up immediately.
Common mistake — testing yourself with the course open

Glancing at the answer "just to check" removes precisely the effort that produces the effect. Recognising an answer and retrieving it are two different operations, and only the second strengthens the memory.

The uncomfortable moment when you search without finding is not a failure of the method: it is the method.

💡 If your revision session is comfortable, it is probably not doing much.

🪞 The illusions of mastery

The real obstacle is not the difficulty of learning: it is the difficulty of knowing what you know. Our judgements about our own mastery are systematically too optimistic, and they are at their most optimistic just after studying.

Fluency mistaken for knowledge

A text read four times reads easily. The brain reads that ease as a sign of mastery — when it only measures familiarity with the layout and the turn of the sentences.

This is why highlighting and rereading remain the most widespread methods: they are the most rewarding at the time. The bill arrives later, when you have to produce without the text in front of you.

The test that settles it

Take a topic you believe you have mastered. Close everything. Write what you know about it, in ten lines, looking at nothing.

What comes out is what you know. The rest was recognition: you would have identified it in a list, you could not produce it.

This test is unpleasant the first time. It is also the only reliable information you have before the exam.

Difficulties that help

A counter-intuitive idea in the field: what makes learning slower and more painful at the time often makes it more durable. Spacing, self-testing, mixing topics — all three degrade immediate performance and improve performance at a distance.

The reverse holds too: the methods that give the impression everything is going in easily are the ones that leave nothing behind.

Common mistake — judging a method by how it feels

"This method doesn't suit me, I'm retaining less" is said almost always in the first few days — at the precise moment when every good method performs worse than rereading.

A learning method is not judged by the session: it is judged a week later, by what remains.

💡 The feeling of learning and the fact of learning do not move in the same direction. That is the central trap of the field.

📚 Going further

A useful habit: at the end of your next reading session, close the page and write three sentences from memory. The contrast with what you thought you had retained is instructive — and it already counts as a review.
