🎓 Learning how to learn — Advanced

What transfers, what can be shown, and what lasts.

⚡ Key points

  • In a language, the first thousand words cover 80% of a text; the next thousand, ten times less.
  • What is learned in a single setting stays attached to it: transfer is prepared, it is not automatic.
  • A method tested right after the session proves nothing — the delay is what separates them.
  • Learning styles and the "10% of what we read" pyramid have no empirical support.
  • What lasts does not depend on motivation: a fixed slot and a five-minute version beat an ambitious plan.

Introduction

The first two levels were about techniques. This one is about the decisions around them: in what order to learn, what will be of use elsewhere, how to sort what is offered to you, and how to keep going over time.

This is the level where you stop applying methods and start judging them — including the ones in this guide.

Warning

This guide is educational. The lexical coverage figures are orders of magnitude: they depend heavily on the language, on the kind of text, and on how you count a "word".

The methods set aside here are set aside on the current state of published work, which is not a final truth.

Deciding what to learn, and how to check it

🗣️ Frequency, or the order in which to learn

In any language, a few words come up constantly and the vast majority almost never. This asymmetry is so strong that it ought to dictate the order of learning — and it rarely does.

The diminishing returns of vocabulary

The 1,000 most frequent words cover about 80% of an ordinary text. The next thousand add only five points. The five thousand after that, four more points.

In other words: the first thousand words pay as much as the next nine thousand combined. Learning vocabulary in alphabetical order, or in the order of a themed textbook, means ignoring this structure.

Worked example — on a page of 250 words

1,000 words known: 80% coverage, that is 50 unknown words per page — one in every five. Unreadable.

2,000 words: 85%, that is 37 unknown. Still unreadable.

5,000 words: 92%, that is 19 unknown. Heavy going.

8,000 words: 96%, that is 10 unknown. Readable, with a dictionary at hand.

Fluent reading needs about 98% coverage, so on the order of 10,000 words. That is a long way off, and above all much further off than it seems at 2,000.

What this changes in practice

Two opposite and equally useful consequences. The first: start with frequency lists rather than with the vocabulary of a theme, as long as you are below 2,000 words.

The second: beyond that, reading accessible texts becomes more profitable than lists, because it brings words in their context — and context is what distinguishes the uses of one and the same word.

Common mistake — aiming for full comprehension too early

At 85% coverage a text remains incomprehensible despite the impression of "knowing nearly all the words". The missing 15% often carry the meaning.

This gap explains the frequent discouragement of beginners who take on a novel: the problem is not their level, it is the choice of text.

💡 This reasoning does not apply only to languages. In any field, a small fraction of the ideas covers the majority of cases.

🎯 Transfer

Transfer is the ability to use elsewhere what you learned here. It is the implicit goal of all learning — and it happens far less often than people assume.

Why what you learn stays put

A memory is stored with its setting: the format of the exercises, the vocabulary used, the room, the time of day. When the setting changes, the memory does not present itself — not because it has gone, but because nothing calls for it.

This is what explains the pupil who can solve an equation in a maths lesson and is stuck in front of the same equation in physics. The knowledge is there; the cue that triggers it is not.

The case of brain-training games

The sales argument rests on distant transfer: training on memory exercises would improve memory in general.

Large studies show something else: players get better at the games, slightly better at very close tasks, and not measurably better at anything else.

This is not an anecdotal result: it is the general rule of transfer, of which these games are an expensive illustration.

What helps transfer

  • Vary the contexts. The same idea met in four different settings detaches itself from each of them.
  • Practise in the conditions of use. To speak a language, speak it; to sit an oral exam, practise standing up and out loud.
  • Go back to the principle. A recipe does not travel, a principle does — hence the use of the question "what is this a particular case of?".
  • Remove the cues that will be missing on the day: the chapter heading, the formula on display, the announced type of exercise.
Common mistake — practising in easier conditions than the real thing

Revising for an oral by reading it in your head, preparing for an interview by vaguely thinking about it, learning a language only through written exercises: in all three cases the practice is on something other than what will be asked.

The rule is blunt and simple: you get good at what you practise, not at what resembles it.

💡 Transfer does not happen on its own. What is learned in a single setting stays attached to that setting.

🔬 Judging a method

The learning field is saturated with methods, many of which rest on nothing. Being able to sort them is worth more than knowing ten more of them.

The three questions that suffice

  1. Compared with what? A method that "works" must do better than another method, not better than doing nothing.
  2. Measured when? This is the decisive question. Almost every pleasant method wins the immediate test and loses the one a week later.
  3. Measured how? What participants felt does not predict what they retained — often the two run in opposite directions.
Three very widespread claims, with no support

"Everyone has their learning style — visual, auditory, kinaesthetic." The idea is popular among teachers. Studies that test it properly — matching the teaching to the supposed style and measuring the result — do not find the announced effect.

"We remember 10% of what we read, 90% of what we teach." This pyramid has circulated for decades with round numbers and no identifiable source. The figures were never measured.

"Listening to Mozart makes you smarter." The original study showed a very limited effect, on one specific task, for a few minutes. The rest is a media stampede.

A useful signal: discomfort

The methods that survive scrutiny — spacing, self-testing, interleaving, elaborating — have one thing in common: they make learning harder at the time.

That is no coincidence. Retrieval effort is precisely what strengthens the trace. A method promising retention without effort is therefore promising something that contradicts the known mechanism.

Common mistake — feeling safe because you know the trap

Knowing that rereading deceives does not stop you finding it effective when you do it. The illusion of fluency does not dissolve through knowing it exists.

Only a measurement corrects it: test yourself at a distance, and look at the result rather than the impression.

💡 Always ask: measured how long afterwards? Most attractive methods do not survive that question.

📐 Building your system

All the techniques in this path share one flaw: they assume you think of them. But motivation falls, weeks get heavy, and a method that requires thinking of it every day eventually gives way.

The five parts

  1. A single entry point. One place where anything you want to remember lands. Three notebooks in parallel guarantee that none is up to date.
  2. Questions, not notes. What enters the system is phrased as a question; otherwise, on the day, you will reread instead of testing yourself.
  3. A spacing schedule, however rough. Software keeps it, so do five dividers.
  4. A fixed slot. What is not in the calendar does not happen. Fifteen minutes daily beats two hours on Sunday, by a long way.
  5. A five-minute version for impossible days. Its purpose is not to make progress, it is to keep the streak unbroken.
What this system looks like in practice

Something learned today goes in the same evening as three questions. It comes back tomorrow, in three days, in a week, then spaces itself out.

The daily session takes fifteen minutes and calls for no decisions: the schedule says what to review. On busy days it drops to five minutes on the overdue cards only.

None of these steps requires motivation. That is exactly what is asked of it.

The review, without which the rest goes stale

A learning system drifts like any other: cards become useless, questions are badly framed, abandoned topics keep taking up the schedule.

A quarter of an hour a month is enough: delete what no longer serves, rephrase what always comes back wrong — a card failed ten times is not difficult, it is badly written.

Common mistake — putting everything into the system

It is tempting to pour every piece of information you meet into it. The schedule then becomes unmanageable, sessions get longer, and the system is abandoned wholesale.

A useful test before adding something: will I need this from memory? If the answer is no, it belongs in a note you can look up, not on a card to memorise.

💡 A system is judged by what is left of it on a Tuesday evening in November, not by its elegance on paper.
