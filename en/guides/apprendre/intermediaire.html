🎓 Learning how to learn — Intermediate

Organising your work, and letting the night finish it.

🕒 Last updated: 5 September 2026

📑 Contents

  • Introduction
  • 🔀 Interleaving
  • 🧩 Elaboration
  • 🎯 Deliberate practice
  • 😴 Sleep and consolidation

⚡ Key points

  • Grouping exercises by type trains you to apply a method, not to choose one.
  • An isolated idea is forgotten; tied to ten others, it can be found by ten routes.
  • Time spent on what you already get right maintains; it does not improve.
  • Consolidation happens during sleep: an all-nighter trades the filing away for more rereading.
  • All four methods in this level degrade immediate performance. That is the sign they work.

Introduction

The beginner level was about when to come back to an idea, and how to work on it: space it out, test yourself, distrust the feeling of mastery.

This level is about the organisation around it: in what order to work, what to do with the content while studying it, where to put your effort, and what happens once the book is closed.

Warning

This guide is educational. The numerical gaps quoted come from experiments run on specific material: they illustrate the size of an effect, they do not transfer as they stand to every subject.

The sleep questions raised here concern ordinary healthy habits. An established sleep disorder calls for medical advice.

Organising the effort

🔀 Interleaving

2 min The usual way of practising is to do twenty exercises of the same type in a row, then move on to the next type.

The usual way of practising is to do twenty exercises of the same type in a row, then move on to the next type. It is comfortable, it gives the impression of mastery — and it is markedly less effective than mixing them.

Why grouping deceives

When every exercise is of the same type, you never have to identify which type it is. You apply the method you have just read, and it comes out right. The series flows, the sense of competence rises.

On the day of the test, nobody announces the type. Yet recognising the problem is precisely the hard part — and the only one that has not been practised.

Worked example — 48 exercises, four types

Blocked — twelve exercises of each type in a row: about 10 passed out of 48 on the delayed test.

Interleaved — the types shuffled: about 30 out of 48, that is 21 more.

Same number of exercises, same time. Only the order changes.

What it costs

Interleaving is unpleasant: you make more mistakes, you go more slowly, you have the distinct feeling of doing worse. That feeling is accurate — at the time. It reverses on the delayed test.

In practice: after seeing a method, do two or three grouped exercises to understand it, then move straight to a mix of everything seen so far.

Common mistake — confusing interleaving with flitting about

Interleaving means alternating exercises within one domain that resemble each other enough to be confused. It is precisely the possible confusion that makes the exercise useful.

Switching every ten minutes between three unrelated subjects is not interleaving: it is task switching, with the restart cost that implies.

💡 Grouping trains you to apply a method. Mixing trains you to choose one — and that is what is asked of you on the day.

🧩 Elaboration

2 min A piece of information on its own is poorly retained.

A piece of information on its own is poorly retained. The same one, tied to what you already know, becomes reachable by several routes — and only one of them needs to work for you to find it.

The habit: “why?”

The technique fits into one question asked of every important claim: why is this true? Then: how does it fit, or fail to fit, with what I already know?

It sounds trivial. It is nonetheless what separates a reading that leaves a trace from a reading that scrolls past.

Two ways of reading the same sentence

“Forgetting follows a curve that is very fast at first, then slower and slower.”

Passive reading: highlight, move on. The sentence seems clear, therefore learned.

Elaborated reading: why that shape? What does it imply for when I review? Is it consistent with the fact that I still remember my childhood address? And if it were false, what would I see instead?

The second takes three times longer and leaves ten times more.

Restate, do not copy

Copying out a definition puts nothing through processing: the hand works, not the memory. Restating it in your own words forces you to build the meaning — and reveals immediately whether you have not understood it.

The same logic applies to examples: the one in the course has been read, it proves nothing. An example you found yourself is the proof that you hold the idea.

Common mistake — perfect notes

Neatly recopying a course in colour takes hours, produces a handsome object and leaves little behind: that is copying, not processing.

A useful set of notes is a set of questions, not answers. It is ugly and it works.

💡 What counts is not the time spent on a piece of content, but what you put it through during that time.

🎯 Deliberate practice

2 min A great deal of practice produces no improvement.

A great deal of practice produces no improvement. You can drive for twenty years without driving better than you did after two. What separates practice that improves comes down to three conditions.

The three conditions

  1. Work on what you get wrong, not on what you get right. It is the simplest condition and the least respected.
  2. Stay at the edge of your ability: hard enough to make mistakes regularly, reachable enough to succeed most of the time. Too easy teaches nothing, too hard teaches nothing either.
  3. Get quick feedback. Without knowing what was wrong, you repeat the mistake and consolidate it.
Worked example — 5 hours a week, for 12 weeks

20% of the time on weak points: 1 useful hour a week, 4 hours of comfort. Over twelve weeks, 12 useful hours.

60% of the time on weak points: 3 useful hours a week, that is 36 hours over the period.

Three times the progress for the same total number of hours. This is not a matter of working more.

Why nobody does it

Because it is unpleasant. Redoing what you get right produces a sense of competence; tackling what you get wrong produces the opposite, with the added feeling of getting nowhere.

The useful marker: a good session leaves a trail of mistakes. A faultless session mainly signals that you stayed on familiar ground.

Common mistake — confusing volume with progress

“I spent six hours on it” says nothing. Six hours rereading what you already know and six hours on your weak points are two different activities, only one of which moves the level.

Counting hours is a convenient and misleading measure. Counting corrected mistakes is laborious and honest.

💡 Time spent on what you already master maintains it. It does not improve it, however long it lasts.

😴 Sleep and consolidation

2 min What you learn is not filed away at the moment you learn it.

What you learn is not filed away at the moment you learn it. Consolidation — the passage from a fragile trace to a stable memory — happens largely during the sleep that follows.

The night is part of the revision

One direct consequence: the night after learning is not neutral time, it is working time. Sacrificing it amounts to cancelling part of what has just been learned.

Another, more useful: revising in the evening then sleeping beats revising in the morning for a test the next day, for the same amount of time. The content then goes straight through the phase that fixes it.

The bad bargain of the all-nighter

Staying up three extra hours the night before an exam buys three hours of rereading — the least effective method in the repertoire.

What it costs in exchange: the consolidation of everything worked on during the day, the next day's attention, and the ability to retrieve what you do know.

Three hours of the worst method against a whole day's filing: the exchange is bad in both directions.

Breaks too

On a shorter scale, breaks play a similar role. A genuine break — no screen, no new demanding task — gives the brain time to replay what has just been seen.

Checking your phone during the break fills that space with something else. That is not a break for memory, it is a change of subject.

Common mistake — believing you can catch up at the weekend

A short night is not fully made up by a lie-in two days later. What was not consolidated at the time is not consolidated retroactively.

Regular hours weigh more than the weekly total of hours slept.

💡 The cheapest review there is happens with your eyes closed. You still have to leave room for it.

📚 Going further

  • “Peak”, Anders Ericsson: the reference work on deliberate practice, by the researcher who framed the idea.
  • “Why We Sleep”, Matthew Walker: on the part sleep plays in memory. Worth reading while bearing in mind that some claims in the book have been disputed.
