🚀 Entrepreneurship — Intermediate

Checking the project stands up before giving it months.

🕒 Last updated: 17 August 2026

📑 Contents

⚡ Key points to remember

  • A project rarely dies of a bad product: it dies of a problem nobody had.
  • A validation interview that talks about the future validates nothing.
  • Price is set on perceived value, never on cost plus a mark-up.
  • The first ten customers almost always come from things that do not scale.
  • A profitable business can die of cash: profit is not the bank balance.

Introduction

The beginner level set the frame: what a business plan is for, how to research a market, which legal form to choose, where to find financing. Enough to start a structure.

This level is about what decides the rest: does the project answer a real need, does it make money on each sale, can you go out and get customers, and does the money last until next month. Four questions whose order is not negotiable — the last kills more businesses than the other three combined.

Warning — please read

This guide is educational. It is neither legal, nor accounting, nor management advice. Thresholds, rates and obligations change: check them at their official source, and get help for the decisions that commit you.

What decides the rest

🎯 Validate before building

The most frequent cause of failure is not a botched product, nor an inadequate team, nor even lack of money. It is having built something nobody really needed.

The interview that validates, and the one that validates nothing

Asking “would you use a service that did this?” produces no information at all. People are polite, project themselves badly, and say yes so as not to disappoint.

A useful interview talks only about the past and the concrete: “Tell me about the last time you had this problem. What did you do? How long did it take you? Did you look for a solution? Did you pay for anything?”

The past can be checked. The future is imagined.

The three signals that really count

The person has already rigged up a solution — a spreadsheet, a notebook, a combination of tools. The problem hurts enough for them to spend time on it.

They have already spent money trying to solve it, even badly.

They ask you when it will be available, without being prompted.

A polite compliment is worth nothing. A “can I pay now?” is worth fifty interviews.

The minimum product, and what it is not

A minimum viable product is not a rushed version of the final one. It is the cheapest way to answer a question.

Often it is not even a product: a presentation page measuring sign-ups, a service delivered by hand for ten customers, a plain spreadsheet. If the question is “will people pay?”, nothing obliges you to build anything to answer it.

Common mistake — validating with the people close to you

Family, friends, former colleagues: they want to encourage you. Their feedback is affectionate and worthless.

Validation requires strangers who have no reason to be kind — and ideally, who take out their card.

💡 What validates a need is not what people say: it is what they have already done about it.

💰 The business model

The business model answers a simple and often postponed question: how do you make money, and how much on each sale? Many projects wait until they have customers before taking an interest. The reverse order is what works.

Setting a price

Three approaches coexist. By cost: add up what it costs and add a margin — simple, and almost always too low. By competition: match the others — restful, and with no advantage. By value: what the customer saves or earns thanks to you.

Only the third lets you be paid properly. If your service saves a professional two hours a week, the reference is not your cost: it is the value of those two hours.

Worked example — margin per unit

Selling price: €49 a month. Variable cost per customer — hosting, support, payment fees: €11.

Margin per unit: €38, that is 78%.

With €4,000 of monthly fixed costs, it takes 106 customers to break even. Not 82, as a calculation on the price on display would suggest.

The trap of the low price

Cutting your price to attract the first customers looks prudent. It is in fact the riskiest choice: you have to sell far more to cover the same costs, the service attracts the customers most sensitive to price, and putting it back up afterwards is very hard.

It is almost always easier to lower a price than to raise it.

Common mistake — forgetting to pay yourself

Many start-up financial plans include no pay for the founder at all. The business looks profitable, and is not: it rests on unpaid work that will not last.

Put your own pay into the fixed costs from the start, even at a modest level. Otherwise you do not know whether the model holds.

💡 A sale that produces no margin is not a customer won, it is a loss that grows with volume.

📈 Finding your first customers

The question that causes the most anxiety, and about which the most bad advice circulates. The first piece to discard: “you need to advertise”.

The first ten do not come at scale

The first ten customers almost always come from things that do not scale: contacting people one by one, travelling to see them, delivering the service by hand, going to an event where they are.

It is inefficient, and it is exactly what you should do. Those first exchanges teach you the customers' vocabulary, their real objections, and what makes up their mind. No advertising campaign supplies that information.

Worked example — acquisition cost

Monthly advertising budget: €800. Visitors generated: 1,600. Conversion rate: 1.5%.

That is 24 customers, for an acquisition cost of €33 per customer.

That figure only means something compared with the margin. If a customer brings in €38 of margin a month and stays six months, the acquisition is comfortably profitable. If they stay one month, it loses money.

One channel at a time

Opening five channels at once guarantees mastering none of them and being able to measure nothing. Better one channel, pushed until you know whether it works, then a second.

The right channel is the one where your customers already are, looking for a solution — not the one that is fashionable.

Common mistake — confusing visibility with customers

Thousands of views, hundreds of followers, and zero sales: a very common case. An audience is not a customer base.

The only indicator that counts at the start is the number of people who pay. Everything else can rise without it.

💡 Do the things that do not scale first. That is where you learn what will need automating later.

🧾 Steering your cash

A profitable business can disappear for lack of cash. It is not a paradox: accounting profit and the bank balance measure two different things.

Why the lag kills

You invoice in January, you are paid in March. In between, wages, rent and suppliers all fall due. That lag between money in and money out is called working capital requirement.

And it rises with growth: the more you sell, the more you advance. A company growing fast can run short precisely because it is growing fast.

Worked example — runway

Cash available: €18,000. Monthly spending: €5,200. Monthly cash in: €3,100.

Monthly cash burn: €2,100. Runway: 8.6 months.

That number is the most important of all. It says how long is left to reach break-even — and therefore from when something else will have to be decided.

Three habits that cost little

Invoice immediately, not at the end of the month. Every day of delay in invoicing is a day of delay in payment.

Ask for a deposit on significant work. It is common practice, and it funds the work instead of you advancing it.

Chase without waiting. An unpaid invoice at 60 days becomes very hard to recover; chased at 5 days, it often settles itself.

Common mistake — confusing revenue with money available

“We did €12,000 this month” says nothing about what is in the account. Part of it has not been received, part belongs to the state — VAT, contributions — and part covers costs already incurred.

The only figure to look at each week is the balance available after deducting what is owed.

💡 Watch your runway in months, not your revenue. It is the runway that says how long is left.

📚 Going further

  • Your country's public business portal: financial plan tools, sector sheets and free support. In France that is Bpifrance Création — the most complete public resource.
  • Your tax and social security authorities: the only up-to-date figures on contributions, VAT and deadlines. Blog articles go stale faster than you think.
  • Chambers of commerce and trade: local support, often free, and knowledge of the economy of your area.
  • “The Mom Test”, Rob Fitzpatrick: the reference book on validation interviews. Short, concrete, and it avoids the costliest mistake.

One useful habit: keep a rolling twelve-month cash forecast up to date. One hour a month, and you see difficulties coming three months ahead.
