🚀 Entrepreneurship — Advanced

The decisions that commit you: growth, other people's money, and people.

🕒 Last updated: 5 September 2026

⚡ Key points to remember

  • Growing with negative unit economics means accelerating towards the wall.
  • A customer must bring in clearly more than they cost to acquire — and fast enough.
  • Raising money is not a success: it is a commitment to fast growth.
  • An employee costs about 1.45 times their gross salary, and far more in management time.
  • The costliest legal risks are born of what was never written down.

Introduction

The intermediate level was about viability: a real need, a margin, customers, cash. Once those four are secured, the business works.

This level is about the decisions that commit you for the long term: whether to accelerate, with whose money, with whom, and under what constraints. Each is hard to undo — and each is taken far more calmly with the right figures in front of you.

Warning — please read

This guide is educational. It is neither legal, nor tax, nor investment advice. The rates and thresholds quoted are orders of magnitude, and they change.

The employer contribution rates in the hiring section are French, as is the trade mark office named at the end. The reasoning — unit economics, dilution, what to write down — depends on no particular country.

For a fundraising round, a hire or an important contract, professional help always costs less than the mistake it avoids.

The decisions that commit you

📊 Unit economics

2 min Unit economics answer a question that growth easily masks: do you make money on each customer, once everything is counted?

Unit economics answer a question that growth easily masks: do you make money on each customer, once everything is counted? As long as the answer is no, every new customer makes things worse.

The two figures, and their ratio

The acquisition cost is what it takes on average to obtain a paying customer: advertising, sales time, introductory discounts, everything included.

The lifetime value is the total margin a customer will bring in before leaving. It is worked out on the margin, never on the revenue — otherwise it is fantasy.

The ratio between the two is the central indicator. A ratio below 1 means you are paying to lose money. A ratio of 3 is generally considered healthy; well above that, it usually means you are not investing enough in acquisition.

Worked example

Monthly margin per customer: €38. Average lifetime: 14 months. Lifetime value: €532.

Acquisition cost: €145. Ratio: 3.7 — healthy.

Payback period: 145 / 38 ≈ 3.8 months. That is how long before the customer has repaid their acquisition cost, and it drives the cash requirement directly.

Why retention dominates everything

Raising the lifetime from 14 to 20 months increases lifetime value by 43% — without spending a single extra euro on acquisition. No advertising optimisation produces such an effect.

That is why work on retention is almost always more profitable than work on acquisition, and almost always done less: it is less visible.

Common mistake — growing to mask a model that does not hold

With negative unit economics, growth solves nothing: it amplifies the loss while giving the appearance of success. Revenue goes up, cash goes down faster.

Until the ratio is clearly above 1, accelerating means pressing the pedal in the direction of the wall.

💡 You do not fix unit economics with volume. You fix them with price, margin or retention.

🤝 Raising money, or not

2 min Raising money is presented as a coronation.

Raising money is presented as a coronation. It is in fact a choice of model, which suits a minority of projects and commits you well beyond the money received.

What you are really selling

You are not selling shares: you are selling a promise of fast growth. An equity investor is looking for a sale or a stock market listing within five to ten years, at a substantial multiple. That assumes a very large market and an aggressive trajectory.

A profitable business growing 15% a year is an excellent affair for its founder, and a bad investment for a fund. The two logics do not meet.

Worked example — dilution

A round of €300,000 on a pre-money valuation of €1,200,000.

Post-money valuation: €1,500,000. The investor holds 20%.

A founder who owned 100% now owns 80%. After two further rounds of comparable dilution, they drop below 52% — and lose control of the decisions that need a majority.

The alternatives, too rarely considered

Financing by customers — deposits, pre-sales, annual subscriptions paid up front — dilutes nothing and validates demand at the same time.

A bank loan, often available with a public guarantee, costs interest but no share and no seat on the board.

Grants and competitions take administrative time and sometimes bring in as much as a small round, with nothing given up in equity.

Common mistake — raising money to find a model

Outside money accelerates what already exists. It does not replace a business model that does not work yet.

Raising before you have viable unit economics means financing a loss for longer, with growth pressure on top. Many failures look like that.

💡 Raising money is not earning it: it is committing to growth in front of people who expect an exit.

👥 Hiring and delegating

2 min The first hire is the moment the business stops being one person's activity.

The first hire is the moment the business stops being one person's activity. It is also the decision most often badly costed.

The real cost of an employee

A gross salary is not the cost. On top come employer contributions — of the order of 40 to 45% of gross in the general French case —, then equipment, training, insurance, and a share of your own hours to manage them.

Hold on to a simple order of magnitude: an employee costs about 1.45 times their gross, not counting the management time of the first months, which is far from negligible. The multiplier differs from country to country; the reasoning does not — check yours before deciding.

Worked example

Monthly gross salary: €2,600. Cost to the employer: about €3,770, that is €45,240 a year.

With a 60% margin, you need to generate about €75,400 of extra revenue a year for this hire to break even.

Put that way, the question becomes concrete: will this person bring in €75,000 of extra revenue, or free up enough of your time for you to bring it in?

Delegate a task, not a problem

Successful delegation assumes the task is defined, documented and measurable. Handing over a vague problem — “take care of marketing” — mostly produces frustration on both sides.

The test beforehand is simple: could you explain in ten minutes what has to be done, and what a good result looks like? If not, the task is not ready to be delegated.

Common mistake — hiring for relief

Being overloaded is not a reason to hire. If the business does not produce the corresponding margin, an employee turns overwork into a cash problem.

Consider first a one-off contractor, an apprentice or part-time work: those are reversible, which a permanent contract is not.

💡 A hire is decided on identified extra revenue, not on a feeling of overload.

⚖️ Legal risks

2 min The legal difficulties of a young company rarely come from an obscure regulation.

The legal difficulties of a young company rarely come from an obscure regulation. They almost always come from what was never written down, at a time when everyone was getting along.

The four points to secure early

The agreement between partners. Who decides what, what happens if one leaves, how the shares are valued, how things split in a disagreement. These questions are easy to settle at the start and become conflicts later.

Intellectual property. What a contractor produces does not automatically belong to you: a written assignment is needed. Without it, the logo, the code or the copy stays with whoever made it.

Terms of sale. Payment deadlines, late penalties, limits of liability, termination conditions. Drafted after a dispute, they are no longer any use.

Personal data. From the very first customer file, the GDPR applies across the European Union: a record of processing, informing the people concerned, retention periods. Outside the EU, an equivalent regime almost always applies — check which.

The calculation that decides

Having articles drafted with a shareholders' agreement: of the order of €1,500 to €3,000.

A conflict between partners with no agreement: several tens of thousands of euros, two years of proceedings, and a company paralysed throughout.

It is one of the rare expenses whose return is measured in multiples.

Common mistake — the partner with nothing in writing

“We're friends, we'll sort it out later.” That is the origin of the majority of conflicts that kill a young company — not competition, not the market.

A disagreement about who put in what, two years later, with nothing written down, is not settled amicably. And it blocks every decision, including a sale.

💡 What costs money is not the contract you draw up, it is the one you did not.

📚 Going further

  • Your public investment bank: loan guarantees, innovation grants, and documentation on equity financing mechanisms. Bpifrance in France.
  • The official employer cost calculator of your social security authority: the official computation, up to date with the rates in force, far more reliable than an approximate ratio.
  • Your data protection authority's controller guide: what to put in place from the very first customer file. In France, the CNIL.
  • Your national trade mark office: registering a mark, protecting creations, and the free prior-rights search — to be consulted before choosing a name. In France, the INPI.
  • Support networks — honour loan schemes, local incubators: interest-free loans and mentoring, often decisive at the first hire.

One useful habit: before any committing decision, write down the scenario where it goes wrong. If that is bearable, go ahead. If not, reduce the commitment.
