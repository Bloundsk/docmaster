📢 Digital Marketing — Intermediate

Understanding where people leave, and why.

⚡ Key points to remember

  • The rates in a journey multiply: four steps at 50% leave 6% at the end.
  • Improving the leakiest step returns more than doubling the traffic.
  • Content that does not match the intent behind the search does not convert, however well it ranks.
  • A metric you cannot tie to a decision is decoration.
  • An address list obtained without consent is a legal risk, not an asset.

Introduction

The beginner level presented the four levers: search ranking, advertising, social networks, email. Each one knows what it does; none says how they fit together.

This level is about what links them: the path a person travels before buying, what they were really after, what can be measured about it, and how to keep the relationship going afterwards. It is the move from “doing things” to “understanding a system”.

Warning

This guide is educational. The rates quoted are orders of magnitude meant to convey a mechanism: they vary enormously by sector, price and audience.

Collecting addresses is governed by data protection law. Check what applies to you before building a list.

Understanding the system

🎯 The buying journey

1 min Nobody buys in one go.

Nobody buys in one go. Between the moment somebody discovers a problem exists and the moment they pay, they cross several steps — and lose people at each one.

The rates multiply

This is the point intuition misses every time. Four steps each keeping 50% of visitors do not leave half at the end, but 6.25%. The loss compounds.

Practical consequence: a step at 20% in the middle of the journey caps everything before it. Bringing in twice as many people changes nothing — the bottleneck stays the bottleneck.

Worked example

1,000 visitors. Product page seen by 40%, added to basket 25%, checkout started 60%, payment completed 50%.

Result: 30 orders, that is 3% overall conversion.

Taking add-to-basket from 25 to 35% gives 42 orders — +40% without a euro more in advertising.

Finding the leak

The useful reflex is not to improve everything, but to spot the step where the rate collapses relative to the others. That is where the gain is, and it is almost always cheaper than buying traffic.

The most frequent causes: a price discovered too late, a compulsory sign-up, an over-long form, or a doubt not resolved — delivery, returns, payment security.

Common mistake — buying traffic to make up for a leaking funnel

Doubling the advertising budget on a funnel at 1% doubles the cost for a proportional result, correcting nothing.

The same budget spent on understanding why 99% leave produces a lasting effect, which then benefits every channel.

💡 Fix the leakiest step before buying more traffic. The gain then multiplies across everything else.

✍️ Search intent

2 min Behind every search sits a precise expectation.

Behind every search sits a precise expectation. Answering it exactly counts for more than keyword density or text length.

Four intents, four kinds of content

Informational — “how to choose an electric bike”: the person is gathering information. A comparison guide fits; a product page will drive them away.

Navigational — “[retailer] electric bike”: they are looking for a specific site. No point trying to rank on it.

Commercial — “best electric bike 2026”: they are comparing before buying. An honest comparison, with criteria, works.

Transactional — “buy folding electric bike”: they are ready. A clear product page, with price and availability.

How to check the intent in thirty seconds

Type the target search and look at what is already ranking. If the first ten results are blog articles, a product page has no chance: the engine has worked out that people want information.

That test costs a minute and saves writing content that could never rank, however good it is.

One piece of content per intent, not one for everything

A page trying to inform, compare and sell at once rarely succeeds at all three. Better a guide that informs and links to the product page, each doing its own job.

Common mistake — going after the most searched queries

“Electric bike” has enormous volume, fierce competition and a vague intent. “Folding electric bike for commuting” has a hundred times less volume — and people who know what they want.

On those specific searches, the conversion rate is in another league, and ranking is realistic.

💡 The best content in the world will not rank if it answers a different question from the one asked.

📈 Measuring what counts

1 min Digital marketing produces a considerable quantity of figures, most of them useless.

Digital marketing produces a considerable quantity of figures, most of them useless. The sorting is done with a single question: which decision can this number change?

Vanity metrics

Impressions, views, followers, reach: they rise with the budget and say nothing about the result. They reassure, they fill dashboards, and they trigger no decision.

The useful metrics are the ones touching the journey: conversion rate per step, cost per customer acquired, average order value, share of new customers.

Knowing where people come from

Adding tracking parameters to your links — source, medium, campaign — lets you tell apart what comes from a newsletter, a post or an ad. Without that, everything blurs into “direct traffic”.

A simple convention, written down and followed, beats a sophisticated system applied at random: source=newsletter and Source=Newsletter will create two different lines.

What cannot be measured, and has to be accepted

Somebody sees a post, mentions it to a friend, who searches the brand name three weeks later and buys. The tool will attribute the sale to the brand search — that is, to nothing.

Part of marketing stays invisible to the tools. Acknowledging it avoids two mistakes: cutting a channel that works, and overrating the one that collects the conversions.

Common mistake — comparing rates without looking at volumes

A channel at 8% conversion on 40 visitors looks better than a channel at 3% on 5,000. The first produces 3 customers, the second 150.

The rate measures effectiveness, the volume measures contribution. Both together, never one alone.

💡 If a figure cannot change any decision, it has no business on your dashboard.

💌 Email and automation

2 min Email remains the channel where you depend least on a platform: the list belongs to you, and nobody can change an algorithm that deprives you of your audience…

Email remains the channel where you depend least on a platform: the list belongs to you, and nobody can change an algorithm that deprives you of your audience overnight.

A list is worth its quality, not its size

A thousand people who asked to receive your messages are worth more than ten thousand bought addresses. The latter produce complaints, degrade your sender reputation — and expose you legally.

Regular cleaning is part of the work: removing addresses inactive for six months improves deliverability for all the other sends.

Worked example — email's funnel effect

A list of 4,000 people. Open rate 32%, click rate on opens 12%, conversion of clicks 6%.

Result: 9 sales per send.

Taking the open rate from 32 to 40% — a better subject line, a better moment — gives 11 sales. The lever is the subject line, not the content.

Automated sequences

Three sequences produce most of the result: the welcome — two or three messages after sign-up, when attention is at its highest; the abandoned basket — a reminder a few hours later; the reactivation — a message to the inactive, before removing them.

They are written once and then work on their own. It is the channel's best effort-to-result ratio.

Common mistake — the pre-ticked box

Signing somebody up to a newsletter through a box ticked in advance, or because they bought something, is not valid consent under data protection law.

Beyond the legal risk, it fills the list with people who asked for nothing: complaints, unsubscribes, and a degraded sender reputation for everyone.

💡 A list built on consent is an asset. A bought list is a liability.

📚 Going further

  • Your national data protection authority's guidance on marketing: what email is allowed depending on whether the recipient is a private individual or a business. The rules differ.
  • Your email tool's documentation: the chapters on deliverability and sender authentication, which decide whether your messages arrive at all.
  • Keyword research tools: to be used first to check the intent behind a search, and only then for the volumes.
  • Sector reports on conversion rates: useful as landmarks, provided you compare comparable sectors and basket sizes.

One useful habit: before writing any content, type the target search and look at the first ten results. They tell you what the engine expects.
