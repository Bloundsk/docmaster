📢 Digital Marketing — Advanced

What the tools do not measure, and what the law governs.

⚡ Key points to remember

  • Last click rewards the channel that collects, not the one that convinced.
  • Taking retention from 88 to 92% a month lengthens the relationship by half.
  • An existing customer costs several times less than a new one, and nobody brings it up in meetings.
  • A persuasion lever stops being legitimate when it rests on false information.

Introduction

The intermediate level was about the system: the journey, the intent, the measurement, the relationship. Enough to run coherent campaigns.

This level is about the blind spots: what the tools attribute badly, what most teams neglect because it is less visible, the line between convincing and deceiving, and the rules that apply whether you know them or not.

Warning

This guide is educational. It is not legal advice: outreach and advertising rules change, and how they apply depends on your business and your country. Consult the official sources, and a professional for anything that commits you.

The blind spots

🧭 Attribution

Somebody sees a post, reads an article, receives a newsletter, then searches the brand name and buys. Who gets credit for the sale? The answer depends entirely on the model chosen — and that choice changes budgets.

Three models, three truths

Last click gives everything to the channel just before the purchase. It is the default setting in most tools, and the most misleading: it systematically rewards brand search and email, which come at the end of the journey.

First click gives everything to the discovery channel. Symmetrically unfair, it values what creates awareness and ignores what convinces.

Distributed attribution spreads the credit across the touchpoints. Fairer, more complex, and still a convention: no model knows what actually went on in the buyer's head.

Worked example — the same journey, three readings

A typical journey: social post → blog article → newsletter → brand search → purchase.

Last click: 100% to the brand search. Tempting conclusion: cut the social budget.

First click: 100% to social. The opposite conclusion.

Distributed: 25% to each of the four touchpoints.

The sale is the same. Three opposite budget decisions can follow from it.

The one check that settles it

Since no model is true, the only way to know what a channel really contributes is to stop it — or cut it sharply — and watch the effect on the total, not on the rows of the table.

It is uncomfortable and it takes patience. It is also the only causal method, exactly like a controlled test.

Common mistake — cutting the channel that does not collect

The discovery channel always shows poor figures under last click. Cutting it mechanically brings down, a few weeks later, the channels that looked like they were performing.

What is then observed is a general fall with no visible cause, and people look for the problem where it is not.

💡 No attribution model is true. The only reliable test is stopping a channel and watching the total.

🔁 Retention

Acquisition takes up most of the budgets and the conversations. Retention produces most of the result, and interests nobody — because it does not show up in a campaign report.

The effect of one point of retention

The average length of a customer relationship follows from the monthly churn rate. Losing 12% of your customers each month gives an average lifetime of about 8.3 months. Bringing that loss down to 8% takes it to 12.5 months.

Four points of retention more, and the relationship lengthens by 50%. No advertising optimisation produces an effect of that size.

Worked example — where the money goes

Acquiring a customer costs €45. Bringing them back costs, depending on the sector, five to seven times less.

A monthly budget of €3,000 spent on acquisition brings in about 67 customers. The same budget spent on reactivating former customers often brings back several hundred.

And yet the second line rarely appears in a marketing plan.

What brings people back

Three levers, in order of increasing effectiveness and cost: keeping your promise — the first order must go exactly as announced; supporting the start — the first few days decide whether it gets used; getting back in touch before you are forgotten — a message at the right moment, not an undifferentiated monthly nudge.

Common mistake — offering more to newcomers than to the loyal

A welcome discount reserved for new customers sends a clear message to those who stayed: their loyalty is worth nothing.

The arithmetic loses twice — you pay more for a less profitable customer, and you demotivate the one who brought in the most.

💡 Four points more retention beat four times the advertising budget.

🧠 Persuading without manipulating

The mechanisms of persuasion have been documented for decades and genuinely work. The question is not whether to use them — all communication does — but where the line runs.

The levers, and their honest use

Social proof. “2,400 customers” informs if it is true and verifiable. An invented counter is a lie.

Scarcity. “Only 3 left in stock” helps somebody decide if the stock is real. A counter that resets on every visit is a deception.

Reciprocity. Offering useful content creates goodwill. It is legitimate as long as the content is genuinely worth something.

Authority. Citing a source or a certification informs. Inventing or exaggerating it deceives.

The test that settles it

One single criterion separates persuasion from manipulation: is the information true, and would the customer be fine with it if they discovered the mechanism?

“Only 3 left in stock” with a real stock of 3: the person would almost thank you for the warning.

The same message with 400 units available: they would feel cheated. The wording is identical; what changes is the truth.

Why the line is also an economic one

Deceptive techniques work in the short term and are paid for afterwards: refund requests, negative reviews, reputation. The gain appears in this month's dashboard; the cost spreads over the following two years, without ever being attributed to its cause.

Common mistake — the fake countdown

An offer “expiring in 2h 14” that restarts on every visit is spotted by part of the audience. Those who spot it will never buy again, and will say so.

A real deadline produces the same sense of urgency without the risk — and lets you repeat it honestly.

💡 Persuasion informs so a decision can be made. Manipulation prevents an informed decision.

⚖️ The legal framework

Marketing is one of the most heavily regulated activities online. The rules apply whether you know them or not, and the penalties fall on the business, not on the contractor who carried the work out.

The description below follows the European framework — the GDPR and the ePrivacy rules. Most countries have an equivalent regime; check the one that applies to you.

Email outreach

To a private individual, the principle is prior consent, freely given and specific. An exception exists for existing customers, on similar products, provided they were able to object at the point of collection.

To a business, at a generic address and about something connected with their job, prior information and a right to object generally suffice. That is not permission to write to anybody.

In every case: the sender's identity visible, a subject line that is not misleading, and one-click unsubscribe, taking effect quickly.

The four obligations people forget

The record of processing activities: it describes what you collect, why and for how long. It is the first thing asked for in an inspection.

The retention period: an address is not kept indefinitely. A period has to be set and applied.

Consent for trackers: audience-measurement and advertising cookies require prior agreement, as easy to refuse as to accept.

Transparency of partnerships: a paid post must be identified as such, including at a creator you work with.

Misleading advertising

A claim has to be provable. “The best”, “number 1”, “guaranteed” commit you, and a price comparison assumes the struck-through price was actually charged.

Environmental claims are watched particularly closely: a vague, unsupported environmental assertion is a misleading commercial practice.

Common mistake — assuming a bought list is legal “because the seller said so”

The controller is whoever uses the data, not whoever sold it. If a complaint is made, it is you who must prove consent.

A list whose origin you cannot demonstrate is a risk nothing makes up for.

💡 In an inspection, it is for you to prove consent — not for the person to prove they never agreed.

📚 Going further

  • Your national data protection authority — marketing outreach: the distinction between private individuals and businesses, and the exceptions for existing customers.
  • Your national data protection authority — cookies and trackers: what makes a banner compliant, and the exemptions for audience measurement.
  • Your national consumer protection body: the rules on claims, struck-through prices and environmental wording.
  • Advertising self-regulation bodies: the professional recommendations, notably on the transparency of paid partnerships.
  • Studies on the cost of retention: the orders of magnitude comparing acquisition and loyalty, by sector.

One useful habit: before launching a campaign, ask yourself what you would say if a customer found out exactly how it works.
