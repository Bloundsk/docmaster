Knowing what a figure does not say, and how many observations it would take to find out.

⚡ Key points to remember

  • Every figure drawn from a sample carries an uncertainty, whether it is shown or not.
  • Halving the margin of error takes four times as many observations.
  • Checking an A/B test every day until it turns favourable guarantees that you will find it favourable.
  • A trend can reverse once the data is pooled: that is Simpson's paradox.
  • Wrong data upstream produces a perfectly presented and perfectly wrong dashboard.

Introduction

The intermediate level covered data processing and the most common errors of reasoning. Enough to produce an honest analysis.

This level covers the question almost nobody asks: how much confidence does the figure deserve? Is a 2% gap between two versions of a page a result, or noise? How many visitors would it take to decide? And what do you do when the answer changes depending on how the data is grouped?

Warning

This guide is educational. The formulas given are the usual approximations, good enough to size a test or judge an order of magnitude — not for a scientific publication.

What a figure does not tell you

🎲 The uncertainty in a measurement

“42% of users prefer version B.” That sentence is incomplete. Out of how many people? Asked how? Without that information the figure is not wrong — it is unverifiable.

Why a sample is enough, and how far it goes

Measuring on part of a population gives an estimate surrounded by a margin of error. The counter-intuitive point: that margin depends almost entirely on the size of the sample, not on the size of the population.

Asking 1,000 people gives roughly the same precision for a city of 50,000 as for a country of 60 million. That is what makes opinion polls possible.

Worked example

On 400 observations, a proportion measured at 50% carries a margin of error of about ± 5 points. The real value therefore sits between 45% and 55%.

To get down to ± 2.5 points you do not need 800 observations but 1,600: halving the margin means multiplying the sample by four.

That is why national polls stop at around 1,000 people. Going further costs a great deal to gain very little.

What the margin of error does not cover

It measures only the randomness of sampling. It says nothing about a badly worded questionnaire, a badly chosen population, or the fact that only the dissatisfied replied. Those biases do not shrink when you ask more people — they grow.

Common mistake — comparing two figures whose margins overlap

Version A: 42%. Version B: 45%. With a margin of ± 5 points on each, the 3-point gap supports no conclusion at all.

Announcing that B wins would be presenting noise as a result.

💡 A percentage without a sample size is not information, it is an impression with a number on it.

🧪 A/B tests

The A/B test is the only everyday method that establishes causation: two groups drawn at random, a single difference, and a comparison of the results. Run well, it settles the question. Run badly, it produces false certainties with all the appearance of rigour.

Size it before you launch it

The question to settle before the test: how many observations does it take to detect the effect you are after? It depends on the starting rate and on the size of the improvement you are aiming for.

The smaller the expected effect, the more observations you need — and the relationship is brutal: detecting an effect half the size takes four times the traffic.

Worked example

Current conversion rate: 3%. Improvement targeted: +20% relative, that is, moving to 3.6%.

You need about 14,000 visitors per group to conclude at the usual thresholds.

If the site receives 1,000 visitors a day, that is four weeks of testing. Knowing it before you launch stops you from stopping after three days believing you have an answer.

Repeated looking, or why you always find what you are looking for

Checking the results every day and stopping as soon as the gap turns favourable is the most common fault. Look often enough and the gap eventually crosses the threshold by chance — then it goes back down.

The rule is simple: fix the duration and the sample size in advance, and only conclude at the end. A test you stop when it agrees with you is no longer testing anything.

Common mistake — testing several things at once

Changing the headline, the button colour and the image in a single version B answers the question “is the whole thing better?” and no other.

If the result is positive, you will not know which element did the work — nor whether one of them makes things worse and is being offset by the others.

💡 An A/B test is sized before it is launched. Afterwards it is too late to ask whether it could conclude at all.

⚠️ Interpretation traps

Three phenomena produce conclusions that are the reverse of reality, starting from perfectly accurate data. Knowing about them costs nothing; ignoring them costs a lot.

Simpson's paradox

A trend present in every group can reverse when the groups are pooled. This is not a mathematical curiosity: it happens as soon as the groups differ greatly in size.

Worked example

Version A: 8% on mobile (800 visitors), 20% on desktop (200 visitors) → average 10.4%.

Version B: 10% on mobile (200 visitors), 22% on desktop (800 visitors) → average 19.6%.

B wins by a wide margin overall. But A and B were not that different: B simply received more desktop visitors, who convert better. B is better in each group as well, but the overall gap — almost ten points — is almost entirely down to how the traffic was split.

Survivorship bias

Analysing current customers to understand why people stay means asking only those who stayed. The ones who left, who hold the answer, have vanished from the file.

Regression to the mean

An exceptionally bad month is usually followed by a better one, even with no intervention. Any action taken after a low point will therefore look effective. It is the most frequent illusion in reviews of corrective action.

Common mistake — concluding without looking at the subgroups

Simpson's paradox only shows up when you segment. An overall result that contradicts every segment is the signal of an unbalanced split, not of a discovery.

The reflex: check that the groups being compared have similar make-ups. Otherwise the overall comparison means nothing.

💡 When the total says the opposite of every part, it is not the total you should believe.

🏗️ The data pipeline

A dashboard is the last link in a chain: collection, transport, storage, transformation, display. An error at any link produces an impeccably presented and wrong result.

The four properties to watch

Freshness. How old is the data on display? A dashboard that does not say leaves you assuming real time. Data frozen for three days because a job failed is more dangerous than no data at all: nobody notices that it has stopped moving.

Completeness. Does a figure below expectations mean a real drop, or a source that failed to report? Telling them apart means counting what is missing too.

Consistency. Two dashboards showing two different figures for “the number of customers” destroy trust in both. The problem almost always comes from diverging definitions that were never written down.

Traceability. For any figure on display, can you trace it back to its source and to the transformation that produced it? Without that, an anomaly cannot be diagnosed.

The cheapest check there is

A simple automatic test on volume: if the number of rows received today differs by more than 30% from the average of the last seven days, an alert is raised.

That trivial check catches the majority of real incidents — a source cut off, massive duplicates, an upstream format change — well before a decision is taken on wrong data.

Common mistake — taking the absence of an alert as a good sign

A job that fails silently leaves the dashboard showing the last known values. Everything looks normal, the figures are plausible, and they are out of date.

Always show the last-updated date next to the figures. It is the cheapest protection there is against the most frequent kind of silent failure.

💡 The quality of an analysis is capped by that of its weakest link, which is rarely the analysis.

📚 Going further

  • Your national statistics office — survey methodology: how public samples are built and which margins of error are published alongside the results.
  • Sample size calculators: to be used before any test, writing down the assumptions you chose. The number you get depends entirely on them.
  • “Thinking, Fast and Slow”, Daniel Kahneman: the chapter on regression to the mean explains why so many corrective actions look effective.
  • Work on Simpson's paradox: the historical cases, notably in university admissions and clinical trials, are more telling than the proof.

One useful habit: before launching a test, write down the planned duration, the planned sample size and the decision threshold. That note will protect you from yourself after three days.
