Introduction

The beginner level covered what can be adjusted: posture, light, breaks. This one covers what has to be understood before it can be addressed — what makes a demanding job sustainable, or stops it being so.

The central distinction is not between “stressful” and “not stressful”. It is between a load that comes back down and a load that no longer does.

Warning — please read

This guide is educational and describes French law where it mentions rules — the minimum rest periods and the right to disconnect are French. Check the rules of your own country. The stress and recovery mechanisms, by contrast, are not country-specific.

The exercises on this page are about working conditions, never about a state of health: none assesses, scores or concludes anything about you.

If something here speaks to you, the person to talk to is a professional: your own doctor, or an occupational doctor.

If you are in distress, contact your national crisis line. In France, 3114 answers free of charge, 24 hours a day.

What makes a load sustainable

🧠 Acute stress, chronic stress

Stress is not a pathology: it is an adaptive response, useful and effective. It mobilises the body against a demand, then subsides. The problem is not that it fires — it is that it does not stop.

The difference is in the return

Acute stress rises before a deadline, a presentation, an unexpected event. It even improves some performance in the moment. Then it falls, and the body recovers.

Chronic stress is what remains when the fall no longer happens. It is not “a lot of acute stress”: it is a different state, with different effects — sleep, mood, concentration, physical health.

The sign that separates the two

A busy period ending in a restorative weekend, then a normal week: that is the expected functioning.

A busy period followed by another, where the weekend is no longer enough, where you think about work at times you did not before, where sleep changes: that is no longer the same thing.

The marker is not the intensity, it is the recovery. And it is more visible from the outside than from within — people around you often notice first.

Why it is hard to see in yourself

The adaptation is gradual. Each week resembles the last, and the point of comparison moves along with the situation. That is what explains sentences like “it is fine, it is just a busy patch” held for eight months.

Hence the use of external markers: a date, a remark from somebody close, a note written six months ago and re-read.

Common mistake — looking for the cause in the person

“I handle stress badly” assumes the load is a given and coping is a skill. But exposure counts at least as much as the way you respond to it.

Organisational factors — load, autonomy, role clarity, support — are documented and are the employer's responsibility. It is not a question of individual robustness.

💡 The marker is not intensity but the return to normal. Stress that no longer comes down is not stronger stress: it is something else.

⚖️ Demand and latitude

Two equally loaded jobs do not have the same effect. What separates them has been documented since the 1980s, and comes down to two dimensions — to which a third is added.

The model

Demand: the amount of work, the deadlines, the pressure, the difficulty.

Latitude: the room left to respond to it. Deciding the order, the method, the moment; using your skills; having a grip on what happens.

Crossing the two gives four situations. Only one is associated with the highest risk: high demand, low latitude. It is the combination, not the demand alone.

The third dimension: support

Added to the original model, it changes the effect of everything else. A strained job where you can ask for help, raise an alarm, be heard, does not have the same consequences as a strained job where you are alone.

The most unfavourable situation described in the literature is the one that combines all three: high demand, low latitude, low support.

That is also practical good news: support is often the most accessible of the three levers.

What the model lets you ask for

Its value is not in filing yourself into a box: it is in naming what is missing. “I have too much work” is hard to discuss. “I have little room on the order of priorities” opens a concrete conversation.

Giving back latitude often costs less than reducing the load — and it is sometimes the only negotiable thing.

Common mistake — treating a passive job as a comfortable one

Low demand and low latitude looks enviable from the outside. It is nonetheless a long-documented wearing situation: disengagement, loss of skills, growing difficulty in getting out of it.

Boredom at work has its own effects, distinct from those of overload, and it is even harder to say out loud.

💡 It is not demand that wears you down, it is demand without room to manoeuvre. And support changes the effect of both.

🔋 Recovery

Recovery is not the absence of work: it is an active process, which needs continuous time. Fragmented, it does not happen — three lots of two hours are not six hours.

The minimums, and what they mean

French labour law sets two rest periods that are not comfort recommendations: 11 consecutive hours between two working days and 35 consecutive hours per week. Other countries set their own; check yours.

In France these are enforceable minimums. Regularly falling below them is not a sign of commitment: it is a situation to report to the occupational health service, and it engages the employer's liability.

Worked example — a 45-hour week

Shortest break between two days: 10 hours. End-of-week rest: 40 hours.

Minimum daily rest: 11 h — yours: 10 h. Minimum weekly rest: 35 h — yours: 40 h.

Finding: below the minimum on daily rest. A single hour's gap, repeated all year, and appearing on no dashboard.

What recovers, and what only looks like it

Three conditions recur in the research: psychological detachment — no longer thinking about it, not just no longer doing it; control — choosing what you do with your time; and an activity that changes register.

An evening spent doing nothing while ruminating about work meets none of the three. It is experienced as rest and produces none.

Common mistake — counting on holidays

The benefits of a holiday are real and documented — and they fade within a few weeks of returning.

A holiday repairs accumulated fatigue; it does not correct an organisation that produces it. With no change on return, the cycle starts again.

💡 Recovery needs continuous time and detachment. Fragmented, or spent ruminating, it does not happen.

🔌 Disconnecting

The right to disconnect has existed in French law since 2017. Its very existence says something: the boundary between work and non-work no longer holds on its own.

What the rule says

Companies must define how that right is exercised — by agreement or by charter. In practice it ranges from no obligation to reply in the evening to firmer arrangements: scheduled sending, servers switched off, alerts.

A point often missed: not replying outside working hours cannot be held against you. That is the very point of the right.

The cascade effect of a late message

A message sent at 11 pm does not only create a load for its author. It signals to the recipients what is expected — even without being asked, even with “do not reply now” added.

That is why scheduled sending, which costs one click, changes a team's habits more than a charter on the wall.

Disconnecting is therefore not only an individual matter: it plays out largely in what each person sends.

Boundaries when working from home

When the place no longer separates, other markers are needed: an end time, a dedicated space however small, a closing ritual — tidying the desk, closing the apps, stepping outside for five minutes.

These are artificial signals, and they work precisely because the brain uses context cues to change mode.

Common mistake — thinking being reachable costs little

Staying available “just in case” without receiving anything has a real cost: the expectation of being contacted prevents detachment, which is the condition for recovery.

It is not the number of messages received in the evening that weighs, it is the possibility of receiving one.

💡 What you send in the evening sets what others believe they must do. It is the most direct lever, and it is in your hands.

📚 Going further

  • Your national occupational safety institute — psychosocial risks: the reference material on organisational factors and their prevention.
  • The Karasek model: the two dimensions of demand and latitude, and the questionnaire used in occupational health since the 1980s.
  • Your national agency for working conditions — resources on workload and on the practical arrangements for disconnecting.
  • Your national crisis line — free and available around the clock in most countries. In France, 3114.

One useful habit: write down on a dated piece of paper how you are at work today, and put it away. Re-read in six months, it will say what memory will not — adaptation erases the points of comparison.
