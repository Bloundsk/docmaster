🎨 UX/UI Design — Advanced

What happens in the user's head, and what to do about it.

🕒 Last updated: 16 August 2026

📑 Contents

⚡ Key points

  • An occupied wait feels shorter than an empty wait of the same length.
  • Decision time grows with the logarithm of the number of options, not linearly.
  • A distant, small target is far slower to reach than a close, large one.
  • A dark pattern produces an immediate conversion and a lasting distrust.
  • A design that is not measured does not improve: it merely changes.

Introduction

The first two levels were about what you draw: screens, navigation, a system. This one is about what happens in the person opposite — how long they think they are waiting, how many options their attention can take, and what makes them give up.

These mechanisms are measurable and have been known for decades. They let you justify a design decision other than by taste, and they raise a question nobody escapes: use this knowledge to help, or to manipulate?

Warning

This guide is educational. The laws quoted are simplified models, useful for reasoning about orders of magnitude — not for predicting an individual's behaviour.

The deceptive practices described in the dark patterns section are presented so that they can be identified and avoided. Several are penalised under European law.

What plays out in the user

⏱️ Perceived performance

Measured time and felt time are two different things. A page that loads in two seconds can feel slower than one that takes three — it all depends on what happens during the wait.

The thresholds of perception

Three values structure this whole area:

0.1 second — the reaction feels instantaneous. Below that, the user has the impression of manipulating the object directly.

1 second — the train of thought is not broken, but the delay is noticed. No visual feedback is needed.

10 seconds — the limit of attention. Beyond it, the person moves on to something else, and you need to show progress to have any hope of keeping them.

Worked example

A page of 2,500 kB on a mobile connection at 10 Mb/s takes about 2 seconds to transfer — plus latency and rendering time.

The same page cut to 800 kB drops under a second. The gain does not come from a faster server, but from the weight removed: uncompressed images, unused fonts, scripts that never run.

Occupying the wait

An empty wait feels about twice as long as an occupied wait of the same length. Hence three effective techniques:

Content skeletons — those grey shapes that outline the layout — give the impression that loading has begun.

Immediate feedback: a button that changes state on click avoids doubt and the second click.

Optimistic display: showing the expected result before the server confirms, correcting it if it fails. This is what makes modern messaging apps feel so fluid.

Common mistake — the infinite spinner

A loading animation with no indication of progress informs of nothing. It only says "something may be happening", and feels all the longer for being indeterminate.

A progress bar, even a rough one, is better tolerated. And for a short operation, a skeleton beats a spinner.

💡 Cutting weight improves real time. Occupying the wait improves felt time. Both count.

🧠 Cognitive load

Every interface demands mental effort: understanding, choosing, remembering. That effort is limited, and it gets spent. An interface that consumes too much tires people, then makes them give up — often without their being able to say why.

Hick's law: the cost of choosing

The time needed to decide grows with the logarithm of the number of options. Concretely: going from 2 to 4 choices costs as much as going from 4 to 8.

One clarification is needed, because it is almost always left out: this law describes choosing between options that are already known. Applied as it stands, it even concludes that a flat list beats a two-tier navigation — since the latter makes you decide twice.

What makes grouping win is something else: visual scanning. A visitor discovering a menu is not choosing, they are searching — and that run costs roughly linearly. Twenty entries to scan, against four groups then five entries: the gap becomes clear.

Fitts's law: the cost of the gesture

The time to reach a target depends on its distance and its size. A small distant target is slow to aim at; a large close one is immediate.

Several well-established consequences follow: the edges and corners of the screen are privileged zones, because the cursor stops there by itself. On a phone, the bottom of the screen is more reachable by the thumb than the top — which explains navigation bars moving downwards.

And the main action of a form must be large and close to the last field filled in, not banished to a corner.

How many items are held at once

Working memory holds a few items at a time — the commonly accepted order of magnitude is around four, not the seven long quoted.

This is why a phone number is read in groups, why a long form gains from being split into steps, and why asking someone to remember information from one screen to the next is a bad idea: show it again.

Common mistake — confusing simple with minimal

Removing labels, hiding options behind obscure icons, deleting explanations: the screen looks cleaner, the mental load goes up.

The user now has to guess. A simple interface is not the one that shows least, it is the one that takes least effort to understand.

💡 The number of options is not as tiring as the absence of a structure for scanning them.

🎯 Dark patterns

A dark pattern is an interface designed to obtain from the user what they would not have chosen knowingly. These are not clumsy mistakes: they are deliberate design decisions, often tested and optimised.

The most widespread forms

Unbalanced consent. "Accept all" as a big coloured button, "manage settings" as a small grey link. Technically the choice exists; in practice it is steered.

One-click subscription, ten-click cancellation. Signing up takes thirty seconds; cancelling means finding a hidden page, then justifying your departure.

Fees revealed at the end. The displayed price is not the price paid. The extras appear after time has been invested in the checkout.

Shaming the refusal. "No thanks, I'd rather pay more." The wording of the refusal is written to be unpleasant to click.

False urgency. A countdown that resets, "3 people are looking at this item" with no basis at all.

What European law says

Several of these practices are not merely questionable: they are illegal.

Consent must be as easy to refuse as to give — a banner with no "reject all" button at the same level as "accept all" is non-compliant.

Cancellation must be possible by a means as simple as subscription. And consumer-protection authorities penalise misleading price display.

Why it is a bad bet

These techniques work: they really do raise conversions in the short term. That is what makes them tempting, and what makes them look effective on a dashboard.

What the dashboard does not show: the later cancellations, the refund requests, the negative reviews, and the distrust that settles in. The gain is immediate and measured; the cost is deferred and diffuse.

Common mistake — the unintentional dark pattern

A box pre-ticked "for simplicity", a refusal button made less visible "for the aesthetics": nobody meant to deceive, and the result is the same for the user.

The test is simple: if the user discovered this mechanism, would they feel cheated? If so, intent changes nothing.

💡 An interface that obtains a consent nobody would have given knowingly has not persuaded: it has trapped.

📐 Measuring design

"It's prettier" is not an admissible argument, and "the client prefers it" even less so. Design can be measured — imperfectly, but well enough to decide.

The three basic measures of a usability test

Success rate: what proportion of participants finish the task unaided. It is the most important measure, and the most often forgotten.

Task time: how long it takes them. To be read with care — faster is not always better, particularly on an important decision.

Number of errors: how many times they get it wrong, go back, or visibly hesitate.

To which is added a standardised satisfaction questionnaire, whose main merit is being comparable from one version to another and from one product to another.

The calculation that convinces a management team

An improvement saves 5 seconds on a task carried out 3 times a day by 200 people, 220 days a year.

That is 5 × 3 × 200 × 220 = 660,000 seconds, about 183 hours a year.

This calculation turns an aesthetic argument into a budgetary one. It is often the only one that gets heard.

What the measures do not say

A usability test shows where people fail, rarely why. The answer comes from observation and from the questions asked afterwards, not from the results table.

And an isolated figure is worth nothing: it is the comparison — before and after, version A and version B — that produces the information.

Common mistake — measuring what is easy to measure

Time spent on a page is easy to collect. It is also perfectly ambiguous: is it interest, or confusion?

A convenient but uninterpretable indicator is worse than no measurement at all: it gives the impression of knowing.

💡 Without measurement, a design does not improve — it changes, and you believe you improved it.

📚 Going further

  • European guidance on deceptive interfaces: what is required of a consent banner, and what is penalised.
  • Consumer-protection authorities: the rules on price display and cancellation, binding on any trading site.
  • Nielsen Norman Group's work: the perception thresholds for response time and the usability test protocols, documented since the 1990s.
  • Performance audit tools: they give the real weight of a page and the detail of what slows it down. The list is almost always dominated by images.
  • The cognitive load literature: Miller's and Cowan's work on working memory, the origin of the grouping rules.

A useful habit: before defending an improvement, put it in hours saved per year. The aesthetic argument is arguable, the arithmetic much less so.
