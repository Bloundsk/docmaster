🎨 UX/UI Design — Intermediate

Designing for everyone, on every screen, without redoing it all on every page.

⚡ Key points

  • Accessibility is not an option for a minority: it benefits everyone first.
  • A menu neatly arranged along your org chart stays incomprehensible to your visitors.
  • Designing for the small screen first forces prioritising; the reverse leads to amputation.

Introduction

The beginner level separated UX from UI, and covered wireframes, visual principles and user testing. Enough to design a screen that stands up.

This level is about what appears as soon as a project grows: users who are not like you, dozens of screens that must hold together, and screen sizes from phone to monitor. Three problems that do not arise on an isolated mock-up, and that decide everything on a real product.

Warning

This guide is educational. The thresholds quoted — contrast, target sizes — come from current accessibility recommendations, but those evolve: check the version that applies to your project.

In France, digital accessibility is a legal obligation for public services and for companies above certain thresholds. Most countries have equivalent rules; check the ones that apply where you are.

What appears when the project grows

♿ Accessibility

Accessibility means designing interfaces usable by people whose sight, hearing, motor control or attention differ from the average. It is imagined to concern a small minority. That is wrong on two counts.

Disability is often temporary or situational

Someone holding a child in one arm uses the screen one-handed. Someone on a train in bright sun can no longer make out weak contrasts. A plastered arm, an ear infection, a phone in battery-saving mode: all of these temporarily put you in the position accessibility accounts for.

Add ageing to that: long-sightedness affects the vast majority of people over fifty.

The four points that settle most of it

Contrast. Text must stand out clearly from its background. The common threshold is a ratio of 4.5 to 1 for normal text, 3 to 1 for large headings. Light grey on white, very fashionable, fails systematically.

Target size. A touch target must measure at least 44 CSS pixels on a side, that is about 9 millimetres — the width of a finger.

The keyboard. Every action possible with a mouse must be possible with a keyboard, and the focused element must be visible. Removing the focus outline because it is judged unsightly makes the site unusable for anyone who cannot aim.

Text alternatives. An image that carries information needs a description; a purely decorative image must be explicitly marked as such, so that it is not read out pointlessly.

What accessibility gives everyone

Subtitles were designed for deaf people. Today they are used mainly by people watching a video with the sound off, on public transport or in a meeting.

Dropped kerbs were mandated for wheelchairs. They mostly serve pushchairs, suitcases and delivery drivers.

Designing for the difficult cases produces an interface that is better for the ordinary ones. The reverse is never true.

Common mistake — leaving accessibility to the end of the project

Picked up at the end, it becomes an overhaul: contrast means revisiting the brand palette, keyboard navigation means restructuring the markup.

Taken into account from the mock-ups onwards, it costs almost nothing — it is mostly a matter of choosing the right colours and sizes from the start.

💡 Designing for difficult situations improves ordinary use. The converse is false.

🧭 Information architecture

This is how content is organised, named and linked. Invisible when it is good, it explains most of the "I can't find it" when it is bad — and no amount of graphic work makes up for it.

The org-chart trap

The temptation is to model navigation on the internal structure of the organisation: one menu per division, one tab per department. It is perfectly logical from inside, and perfectly opaque from outside.

A visitor is not looking for "the membership department": they are looking for "how do I sign up". Navigation must follow the users' tasks, not the hierarchy of the house.

Card sorting

The simplest method for settling it: write each piece of content on a card, ask five or ten people to group them as feels natural to them, then to name each group.

The groupings that recur across most participants indicate the expected structure. The cards everyone places somewhere different signal content that is badly named, or that has no obvious home.

Labels count as much as structure

"Our solutions" says nothing. "Pricing" says everything.

"Customer area" and "My account" on the same site create hesitation at every visit: the visitor has to guess which of the two concerns them.

A label is judged on one thing only: does someone new to the site know what they will find behind it? In-house vocabulary almost always fails that test.

Common mistake — adding a menu entry for every request

A menu grows by one entry for every new need expressed, until it has fifteen. Each was added for good reasons; the whole has become unreadable.

Beyond about seven entries, grouping is needed — and the grouping must come from users, not from an internal arbitration.

💡 Navigation is designed from the visitors' tasks, never from the org chart.

📱 Designing mobile first

Most web traffic today happens on phones. Yet many projects still design the large-screen version first, then "adapt" it. The order is not neutral.

Why start with the small screen

A phone screen forces you to choose. There is no room for three columns, a banner, a sidebar and six calls to action. Designing for that constraint first forces you to identify what really matters.

Widening afterwards is easy: you have more space for what has already been judged essential. The reverse — starting from the large screen then cutting — leads to trade-offs made in a hurry, and to amputated mobile versions.

The rule for touch targets

A finger covers about 9 millimetres. Recommendations set the minimum size of a target at 44 CSS pixels, with enough spacing between neighbouring targets.

A 24-pixel link in a footer, surrounded by other tightly packed links, guarantees handling errors — and the user will think they got it wrong themselves.

Breakpoints follow the content

The classic mistake is setting breakpoints from the phone models of the moment. They change every year, and there are hundreds of them.

The right method: widen the window gradually and place a breakpoint where the layout starts to age badly — a line of text that runs too long, an ungainly empty space. The content dictates, not the catalogue.

Common mistake — hiding instead of prioritising

Hiding half the elements behind a menu on mobile is not responsive design: it is postponing the decision.

If a piece of content does not deserve its place on a phone, the real question is whether it deserves it on a large screen.

💡 The small screen does not shrink your interface: it reveals what had no place in it.

🧱 The design system

Past about twenty screens, a product designed page by page drifts: seven shades of blue, four button styles, three ways of showing an error. Nobody decided it — each screen was simply made separately.

What a design system really is

It is not a component library. It is a set of decisions taken once: the palette, the type scale, the spacing, the states of a button, the way an error is signalled. Components are only the embodiment of those decisions.

A system is useful precisely because it closes questions. Every time a designer wonders "what size for this heading?", the system should have answered beforehand.

A scale rather than free values

Without a system: spacing comes out at 12, 15, 18, 20, 24, 25, 30 pixels depending on the screen and the day. Nothing is outright wrong, the whole looks slapdash.

With a scale — 4, 8, 16, 24, 32, 48 — the choice narrows to a few options, and consistency becomes automatic.

The constraint saves time: fewer decisions to take, and a more regular result.

Design debt

Like technical debt, it builds up through small exceptions: "just for this screen", "the client wanted a different button". Each looks harmless; their sum makes the system unusable, since it no longer describes the real product.

An accepted exception must be either absorbed into the system or fixed. Leaving it to live alongside is the beginning of the end.

Common mistake — the system with no owner

A system nobody maintains becomes wrong within months: the documentation describes one version, the product shows another, and teams stop consulting it.

A design system is a product in its own right. Without someone to arbitrate it, it does not survive.

💡 A design system is not there to look nice: it is there so that the same decisions are never retaken.

📚 Going further

  • WCAG — the international standard, where levels A, AA and AAA are defined. Most national rules derive from it.
  • RGAA — the French accessibility framework, with its criteria and tests. It is the document that counts in a French audit.
  • Online contrast checkers: two colours, a ratio, a verdict. Use one when choosing the palette, not afterwards.
  • Public design systems — those of governments and large platforms: they show the level of detail expected, and are freely available.

A useful habit: navigate your own site with the keyboard alone, no mouse. Five minutes are enough to reveal most accessibility problems.
