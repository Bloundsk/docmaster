🔒 Cybersecurity — Beginner

Protecting yourself with the basic habits. No technical background needed.

🕒 Last updated: 5 September 2026

📑 Contents

  • Introduction
  • 🔑 Passwords & authentication
  • 🎣 Phishing
  • 🛡️ Firewalls and antivirus
  • 🔐 Data encryption

⚡ Key points to remember

  • Switch on two-factor authentication (2FA) on all your important accounts.
  • Never click straight through a link received by email when in doubt.
  • An antivirus does not replace human vigilance.
  • Always check for the padlock (HTTPS) before entering sensitive information.
  • Never use the same password on more than one service.

Introduction

People readily picture cybersecurity as a matter for experts facing gifted hackers. The reality is far more mundane: the overwhelming majority of compromises exploit no technical flaw at all. They rest on a reused password, a well-imitated email, or software that was not updated.

The good news: that means a few simple habits remove most of the risk. This is not about becoming invulnerable — no such thing exists — but about no longer being an easy target.

This guide covers the four subjects that protect the most: authentication, recognising manipulation attempts, the protection tools and their real limits, and encryption.

The basics to know

🔑 Passwords & authentication

2 min The password guards access to everything else.

The password guards access to everything else. And the main problem is not how complex it is — it is reuse.

Why reuse is the real danger

Sites get breached regularly, large platforms included. The credential lists that come out then circulate freely. Programs replay them automatically against hundreds of other services: this is credential stuffing.

The real scenario

You create an account on a small forum in 2019 with your usual address and your usual password. The forum closes, its database leaks.

Today, a program tries that pair on your email, your bank, your social accounts, your online shops. No sophisticated hacking: a plain list and a script.

If your email falls, everything else follows — password resets go through it.

The site haveibeenpwned.com lets you check for free whether your address appears in known breaches. The answer is often yes, and it is instructive.

What makes a good password

Length counts for more than exotic characters. A phrase of four unrelated words holds up better than a short word stuffed with symbols, while staying memorable.

ExampleStrengthWhy
P@ssw0rd!Very weakThe classic substitutions are in every attack dictionary
Bristol1987WeakCity and year of birth: findable on your social accounts
turtle-stapler-cloud-vinegarStrongSubstantial length, no predictable logic

Two-factor authentication

2FA adds a second proof: a temporary code, a physical key. Even with your password, an attacker is blocked. It is the measure with the best effort-to-protection ratio.

Not all methods are equal:

  • Physical key (YubiKey, Titan) — the safest, resists phishing too.
  • Authenticator app (Authy, Google Authenticator) — an excellent compromise, free.
  • SMS — better than nothing, but vulnerable to SIM swapping: a fraudster persuades the operator to move your number to their SIM card.

Absolute priority: your main mailbox, then the bank, then accounts holding payment methods.

Common mistake — enforced periodic changes

Forcing a password change every 90 days produces the opposite effect: people go from Summer2026! to Summer2026!!. Security agencies no longer recommend the practice.

A long, unique password, changed only after a breach, protects better.

💡 A password manager and two-factor authentication on your mailbox: those two measures, in one evening, remove most of your personal risk.

🎣 Phishing

2 min Phishing does not attack the machine: it attacks the person.

Phishing does not attack the machine: it attacks the person. A trusted organisation is imitated in order to obtain a password, a card number or a transfer.

The mechanism is always the same

Three ingredients, every time:

  1. An authority: your bank, the tax office, a delivery firm, your management.
  2. An urgency: account suspended, parcel held, fine to settle within 48 hours.
  3. An immediate action: click, enter, pay.

The urgency is not a detail: it exists precisely to prevent checking. Any time pressure in a message is in itself the main warning sign.

What goes unnoticed

Spelling mistakes have become rare — automatic writing tools removed that signal. Look instead at the domain, to the right of the @ and before the first /:

  • secure-bank.com — that is not bank.com
  • bank.com.account-check.net — the real domain is account-check.net
  • bnnk.com — one letter changed, invisible at a glance

The HTTPS padlock proves nothing: anybody gets a free certificate in five minutes. It guarantees the connection is encrypted, not that the site is honest.

The variants

  • Spear phishing — a personalised message with your name, your job, a project under way. Far more effective.
  • CEO fraud — a “director” asks for an urgent, confidential transfer. This kind of fraud costs businesses considerable sums every year.
  • Smishing — by text message. Parcels, fines, bank accounts.
  • Vishing — by phone, often posing as your bank's anti-fraud team.

The single reflex

Never use the link supplied. Open the app yourself or type the address you know. If the message was genuine, the information will be waiting in your account.

On the phone: hang up and call the number on the back of your card. A real adviser will understand perfectly; a fraudster will try to talk you out of it.

Common mistake — believing you will not fall for it

Corporate test campaigns show high click rates, technical staff included. The deciding factor is not competence: it is tiredness, workload, or the message arriving just when you happened to be expecting a parcel.

That is why the defence has to be a systematic habit, not occasional vigilance. A habit holds even when attention drops.

💡 No legitimate organisation asks for a password or a verification code, through any channel. That rule has no exceptions.

🛡️ Firewalls and antivirus

2 min These tools are useful and largely sufficient for personal use — provided you understand what they do, and above all what they do not.

These tools are useful and largely sufficient for personal use — provided you understand what they do, and above all what they do not.

What each one protects

ToolRoleDoes not protect against
FirewallFilters incoming and outgoing connectionsWhat you install deliberately
AntivirusDetects known malicious softwarePhishing, a stolen password
UpdatesFix published holesNothing — it is the most effective measure

On Windows, Defender is built in, free, and regularly comes top in independent tests. Paying for a third-party suite generally brings no measurable security gain.

The most effective measure is the most boring one

Installing updates. A publicly disclosed hole is exploited by bots within days. The fix almost always exists before the attack — it is the delay in installing it that creates the vulnerability.

That goes for the system, but also the browser, the extensions, the phone, and the internet router, which everybody forgets.

Ransomware

Ransomware encrypts your files and demands a payment. No antivirus guarantees protection. The only genuinely reliable defence is the backup.

The 3-2-1 rule
  • 3 copies of your important data
  • 2 different media (internal disk + external disk)
  • 1 copy off site, or disconnected

The critical point is the last one: an external disk left permanently plugged in gets encrypted along with the rest. A disconnected backup is a backup.

And a backup never tested is not one: try restoring a file at random, once a year.

Common mistake — false security

“I have an antivirus, I am protected” leads to dropping your guard. But the antivirus sees nothing when you deliberately type your password into a fake site — and that is by far the most frequent scenario.

💡 Automatic updates switched on and a disconnected backup: two settings that protect more than any paid software.

🔐 Data encryption

2 min Encrypting means making information unreadable to anyone without the key.

Encrypting means making information unreadable to anyone without the key. It is the technology that lets you type a card number over a network you do not control.

Encryption in transit and at rest

  • In transit: during the journey. That is the job of HTTPS — the padlock in the address bar.
  • At rest: on the disk. BitLocker on Windows, FileVault on Mac. Essential on a laptop: if it is stolen, the disk stays unreadable.

What the padlock means — and does not

A very widespread confusion

HTTPS guarantees one single thing: nobody can read or alter the exchanges between your browser and that site.

It says nothing about the site's honesty. A fraudulent site has a perfectly valid padlock — certificates are free. Your card number then reaches the fraudster, perfectly encrypted.

The padlock protects the road, not the destination. Always check the domain name.

End-to-end encryption

With end-to-end encryption, only the sender and the recipient can read the content — the service provider itself cannot.

Signal and WhatsApp apply it to messages by default. Many conventional email services do not: your messages there are readable by the host.

Beware of metadata: even encrypted, an exchange reveals who spoke to whom, when and for how long. That information is often as telling as the content.

Public Wi-Fi

The risk is smaller than it used to be, since nearly every site uses HTTPS. Two precautions remain: never join an open network with an over-welcoming name without checking its exact name with the venue, and avoid sensitive operations from an unknown network. Tethering from your phone is safer.

Common mistake — the VPN presented as universal protection

A VPN encrypts the journey as far as its provider. It protects neither against phishing, nor viruses, nor a weak password. It simply moves trust from your internet provider to the VPN provider — who then sees all your traffic.

Useful for getting round a geographic restriction or on a genuinely dubious network. It is not a security shield, whatever their advertising suggests.

💡 The padlock certifies that the road is safe, never that the destination is honest. Read the domain name before trusting the icon.

📚 Going further

  • Your national cybercrime reporting service — most countries run one. Online diagnosis, response sheets, and referral to vetted providers if you are a victim.
  • Your national cybersecurity agency — its computer hygiene guides are the reference, including for very small organisations.
  • Have I Been Pwned — check for free whether your address appears in a known breach, and get alerted to the next ones.
  • Your national data protection authority — your rights over your data, and what to do about a breach affecting you.
  • Phishing reporting services — to report a fraudulent site and get it taken down.

If something has actually gone wrong: change the password of your main mailbox first, from a clean device, switch on two-factor authentication there, then deal with the other accounts. File a report with the police — it is essential for any claim with your bank or insurer.
