🔒 Cybersecurity — Intermediate

Putting in place what lasts, without thinking about it every day.

🕒 Last updated: 16 August 2026

📑 Contents

⚡ Key points to remember

  • A reused password turns a single breach into a general compromise.
  • The mailbox gets protected before anything else: it resets every other account.
  • A second factor by SMS is the weakest of the second factors — but far better than none.
  • A backup left permanently plugged in gets encrypted along with the original.
  • A VPN moves trust to its provider; it does not remove it.

Introduction

The beginner level was about reflexes: recognising a dubious message, keeping your system updated, understanding what encrypting means. Actions to repeat.

This level is about arrangements: things you set up once and that then work without you. That is a change in kind. Security resting on daily vigilance always gives way in the end, on a tired or hurried day. Security resting on a mechanism holds even on the bad days.

Warning

This guide is educational and general. Its recommendations hold for ordinary personal use; a professional, non-profit or exposed setting calls for its own measures and a specialist's advice.

Tools and services change: check the settings on the official pages before acting.

The arrangements to put in place

🔐 The password manager

The advice “a different password everywhere” is correct and impossible from memory. Nobody remembers eighty distinct passwords. The outcome is well known: one gets reused, sometimes two, across the lot.

The real problem is not strength, it is reuse

A long, complex password is still a stolen password if the service holding it suffers a breach. And the lists from those breaches are then replayed automatically against hundreds of other services — email, shops, social accounts. No human attacker is involved: a program tries them, in bulk.

A manager remembers for you. You memorise one single password, the vault's, and each site gets a unique value you do not even know.

Worked example

Somebody has 80 accounts and reuses the same password on 60% of them.

One of those 80 services suffers a breach. 48 accounts become immediately reachable, with no extra effort.

With a unique password per service, the same breach compromises one.

The “all your eggs in one basket” objection

It is legitimate and deserves a straight answer. Yes, the vault concentrates the risk. But it concentrates it on one point you protect seriously: a long password, known to you alone, backed by a second authentication factor.

The situation without a manager is not “risk spread out”: it is the same risk, spread across eighty services whose security and breaches you control not at all.

Common mistake — the browser's own list

Saving passwords in the browser is better than reusing them. But synchronisation is often tied to an account itself protected by a plain password, and access to the open session is sometimes enough to read everything in clear.

If you settle for it, at least protect that account with two-factor authentication.

💡 The manager does not make your passwords stronger: it makes reusing them unnecessary.

📱 Two-factor authentication

A password is something you know. It can therefore be guessed, stolen, or read in a breach. The second factor adds something you have — a phone, a key — that a remote attacker does not.

The three forms, from weakest to strongest

SMS works everywhere and needs nothing installed. It is also the most fragile: SIM swapping lets somebody receive your codes in your place, and the operation is within reach of a motivated attacker. It remains far better than no second factor at all.

An app generates a code every thirty seconds, on the device, with no network. Nothing to intercept: the code travels nowhere. It is the right balance between effort and protection.

A physical key is an object to plug in or hold near. It also checks the site's address, which makes it immune to phishing: on a copycat site it simply refuses to respond.

Where to start

Not all accounts are equal. Your main mailbox comes before everything: it receives the reset links for all the others. Whoever controls it controls the lot, however carefully you protect the rest.

Common mistake — backup codes left in the wrong place

Every service offers backup codes when you switch it on. Many people save them… on the phone that carries the authenticator app.

The day that phone is lost, stolen or replaced, the account becomes unreachable to its rightful owner. Those codes must live elsewhere: printed, or in the password manager.

💡 Start with the mailbox. Everything else depends on it, including what you protect next.

💾 Backups and ransomware

Ransomware encrypts your files and demands a payment for the key. Against it, an antivirus is only one barrier among others. The one answer that works every time is a backup it could not reach.

The 3-2-1 rule

Three copies of the data, on two different kinds of media, one of them outside the home. This rule does not come from the fight against ransomware — it is far older — but it applies perfectly.

What each level covers

Three copies: a disk failure loses nothing.

Two different media: a batch defect or a mishandling does not take everything.

One outside the home: a fire, a flood or a burglary leaves a way out.

And a fourth condition, specific to ransomware: one disconnected copy.

Why the permanently plugged-in disk does not count

Ransomware encrypts everything your session can write to. The external disk left plugged in, the folder synced to the cloud, the network drive: all of it goes, often within minutes. A backup that is writable is not a backup, it is one more copy to encrypt.

The copy that saves you is the one that was unplugged at the time of the attack.

Common mistake — the backup never restored

A system that has said “backup successful” for three years may be empty, incomplete, or unreadable. You only find out when you need it — that is, at the worst possible moment.

Restore a file at random, once a quarter. A backup never restored is only a hypothesis.

💡 Paying the ransom guarantees nothing. An unplugged copy does.

🌐 The network and the VPN

The VPN is the most heavily sold and most misunderstood security product. It does one precise thing, very well; it does not do most of what is attributed to it.

What it actually does

It creates an encrypted tunnel between your device and a server, then goes out onto the internet from that server. Two consequences: whoever runs the local network — the café's wifi, the hotel's — no longer sees which sites you visit, and the sites visited see the server's address instead of yours.

What it does not do

It does not analyse traffic: a phishing site remains a phishing site, a malicious file remains malicious. It does not make you anonymous on services where you are logged in with an account — you identified yourself there. And it does not replace HTTPS: that protects all the way to the site, the VPN only as far as its own server.

The shift in trust

Without a VPN, your internet provider sees the domains you visit.

With a VPN, it sees nothing any more — but the VPN provider sees everything.

The question is therefore not “am I protected?” but “who am I trusting?”. A free service funded by advertising is not the best candidate to receive the whole of your traffic.

Common mistake — the false sense of security

Some providers' advertising suggests a VPN protects against viruses, scams and bank details being stolen. It does none of that.

The risk is real: believing your browser protected leads to dropping your guard where it counts — on the links clicked and the files opened.

💡 HTTPS protects the content everywhere. The VPN hides the destination from your local network. They are not the same problem.

📚 Going further

  • Your national cybercrime reporting service: the public assistance scheme. Practical sheets, and above all what to do when an incident has actually happened.
  • Your national cybersecurity agency: its digital hygiene guides. Written for organisations, but the principles hold for an individual.
  • Your national data protection authority: what to do after a data breach, and what your rights are against the service that suffered it.
  • Have I Been Pwned: check whether an address appears in a known breach. The service is well regarded and asks for no password — if it asked for one, that would be the signal to run.

One useful habit: before installing a security tool, look up who funds it. A free product handling the whole of your traffic has to make its money some other way.
