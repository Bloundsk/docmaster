🔒 Cybersecurity — Advanced

What is left once the obvious protections are in place.

🕒 Last updated: 16 August 2026

📑 Contents

  • Introduction

  • 🎭 Targeted social engineering
  • 🔏 The limits of encryption

⚡ Key points to remember

  • A breach is not visible: on average it is discovered months after the fact.
  • Changing your password is not enough to take an account back — a forwarding rule outlives it.
  • A targeted attack shows none of the crude signs of mass phishing.
  • End-to-end encryption protects the content, never the metadata.
  • Account recovery is often the weakest link, and the least watched.

Introduction

The first two levels covered the habits and the arrangements. Once those are in place, the risk does not disappear: it moves.

It moves towards what you do not control — the databases of the services you use — and towards what no tool filters: a plausible request, addressed to you personally, at the right moment. This level is about those blind spots, and about the real limits of protections believed to be total.

Warning

This guide is educational and general. It describes attack mechanisms so that they can be defended against. A professional, non-profit or exposed setting calls for its own measures and a specialist's advice.

If an incident has actually happened, go to your country's official cybercrime reporting service rather than to an online guide.

The blind spots

🧬 Data breaches

You can do everything right and still be compromised: all it takes is for a service you entrusted an address and a password to have its database stolen. That risk does not depend on you.

Credential stuffing

Once published, the list circulates and gets replayed automatically against hundreds of other services. This is credential stuffing. The success rate is low — a few tenths of a percent — but applied to millions of pairs, it is more than enough.

That your password is long changes nothing: it is not guessed, it is known.

The order of magnitude of the delay

The average time between an intrusion and its detection is counted in months, not days. The time between detection and notifying the people affected often adds several weeks.

In other words: by the time you receive the email telling you about a breach, the credentials have been circulating for a long while. The notification is not an alert, it is a statement of fact.

Taking back control, in the right order

Order matters, because some steps make the next ones pointless if they come too late. The recovery mailbox first: as long as an attacker controls it, they take back everything you have just changed.

Then comes the service concerned, then every service where the password had been reused — and finally, the step almost everybody forgets.

Common mistake — stopping at the password change

An attacker who has had access to a mailbox often leaves an automatic forwarding rule behind: every message received is copied to them. That rule keeps working after the password change, silently, including for future reset links.

Checking the forwarding rules and the active sessions is part of taking back control. The password alone is not enough.

💡 A breach cannot be prevented from your side. What can be prevented is its spread.

🎭 Targeted social engineering

Mass phishing is recognisable: mistakes, a dubious address, a generic greeting. A targeted attack shows none of those signs. It is written for you, from real information, and asks for an action that makes sense in your context.

Where the information comes from

From public sources, most of the time: a professional profile, a job advert, an org chart, a post on social media. Nothing is hacked to build the message — everything is simply read.

The classic form

A message appearing to come from a manager, the day before a long weekend: “I am travelling, this invoice has to be settled today. I am counting on your discretion, I will explain later.”

Three levers act together: authority, urgency, and confidentiality — the last one there to prevent the one thing that would defeat the attack: asking somebody else.

The only reliable defence

It is not reading the message more carefully. A well-made message cannot be told apart from a real one, and believing yourself able to spot the impersonation is precisely what makes you vulnerable.

The defence is procedural: for any unusual request touching money, credentials or data, check through another channel — a number you already knew, never the one given in the message.

Common mistake — believing vigilance is enough

Every study on the subject reaches the same conclusion: trained, forewarned people get caught too, simply less often. Tiredness, workload and context do the rest.

A written rule — “no transfer without voice confirmation” — protects even on the days attention is lacking. That is the whole point of it.

💡 Against a targeted attack, it is not your judgement that protects you, it is your procedure.

🔏 The limits of encryption

End-to-end encryption guarantees one thing, and guarantees it solidly: only the correspondents can read the content. Not the service, not an intermediary, not anybody intercepting the traffic.

What it does not protect is just as important, and far less well known.

Metadata stays visible

Who writes to whom, at what time, how often, from which network address, at what volume: that information travels in clear on most services, encryption or not.

And it is often enough to reconstruct the essentials. Knowing that somebody exchanged messages with an emergency service, then a lawyer, then their bank, in that order and the same afternoon, requires reading no message at all.

What stays exposed despite encryption

The devices. Encryption protects the transport, not the devices. A decrypted message appears on screen, and is sometimes saved in clear.

The backups. An encrypted conversation whose automatic backup is not encrypted becomes readable again in the cloud.

The recipient. Nothing stops your correspondent taking a screenshot, nor their own device being compromised.

Common mistake — the backup that cancels the encryption

Several encrypted messaging apps offer a cloud backup of conversations. If that backup is not encrypted separately, the whole exchange becomes reachable by whoever gets access to the storage account.

The protection of the transport is then intact, and perfectly useless.

💡 Encryption protects what you say. It does not hide who you say it to, nor when.

🗺️ Your attack surface

The attack surface is the set of points through which somebody could get in. It grows on its own, with no decision: every account created, every application authorised, every service tried and forgotten widens it a notch.

Forgotten accounts are the most dangerous

A service signed up to eight years ago, never used since, still holds your address and a password from back then — often one of the ones you were reusing at the time. It has not been updated, offers no two-factor authentication, and you will not be told if it leaks, since you have forgotten it exists.

Account recovery, the weak link

All the attention goes on the password. But most services offer a parallel route: security questions, a recovery address, a phone number.

Those routes are frequently weaker than the front door. A question like “your first pet's name” can sometimes be answered by reading a public profile. An abandoned recovery address can be recreated by a third party if the domain allows it.

Shrinking the surface, once

Take stock: the list of accounts in the password manager, plus a search for “welcome” or “confirm your registration” in your mailbox.

Close what is no longer used. A deleted account does not leak.

Revoke the third-party applications authorised on your main accounts: many keep permanent access long after they stopped being used.

Check the recovery addresses and numbers: they must be yours and still active.

Common mistake — honest answers to security questions

Your mother's maiden name, your town of birth, your first school: that information is public or easy for someone close to you to guess.

A security question is not a sincerity test. Treat the answer as a second password: arbitrary, and kept in the manager.

💡 Every account closed is one door fewer, for good.

📚 Going further

  • Your national cybercrime reporting service: what to do when an incident happens, and referral to vetted providers.
  • Your national data protection authority: your rights after a breach, and the obligations of the service that suffered it — notably the notification deadline.
  • Have I Been Pwned: monitoring an address against known breaches, with an alert if it appears again.
  • Your national cybersecurity agency: guides on authentication and on securing email, more detailed than most general-audience articles.
  • Annual breach reports: detection times and the most frequent points of entry, in figures and without sales commentary.

One useful habit: once a year, re-read the list of applications authorised on your main account. It almost always contains services you had forgotten existed.
