🤖 Artificial Intelligence — Intermediate

What you need to understand to build something that holds up.

🕒 Last updated: 16 August 2026

⚡ Key points to remember

  • A model “remembers” nothing: everything it knows of the conversation is sent back on every turn.
  • RAG does not make the model cleverer: it puts the right documents in front of it.
  • An agent only acts through the tools you give it — that is where what it can break is decided.
  • The best-performing model is rarely the right choice for a simple, repeated task.
  • Cost is worked out per million tokens, not per request: the context weighs more than the answer.

Introduction

The beginner level explained what a language model is, what an agent is, how to phrase a prompt and where the limits sit. Enough to use the existing tools with judgement.

This level is for anyone who wants to build: plug a model into their own documents, give it tools, and make choices that commit a budget. The four notions that follow explain most project failures — not because they are difficult, but because they are rarely understood before they have cost money.

Warning

This guide is educational and deliberately independent of any vendor. The orders of magnitude quoted — context sizes, prices per million tokens — move fast: check them at the source before sizing a project.

The notions to master

🧩 Context and tokens

A model does not process words but tokens: fragments of text, often a syllable or a short word. As a rule of thumb, count about three tokens for every two words — the exact ratio depends on the language and the tokeniser. An 80,000-word novel therefore weighs some 120,000 tokens.

The context window is a ceiling, not a memory

The context window is the amount of text the model can read in one go: the prompt, the documents supplied, the conversation history and the answer to be produced, all added together.

The important point, and the most misunderstood: the model remembers nothing between two calls. What creates the illusion of memory is that the full history is sent back to it on every turn. A thirty-message conversation therefore costs far more at the thirtieth turn than at the first, since everything is re-read each time.

Worked example

A conversation where each exchange is 500 tokens. At the tenth exchange, the history weighs 5,000 tokens.

Over ten turns, the total read by the model is not 5,000 tokens but about 27,500 — the sum of the successive histories. It is that total that gets billed.

What happens when the window overflows

Two behaviours exist depending on the tool: either the call fails, or the start of the conversation is silently truncated. The second is the more treacherous — the model carries on answering, but has “forgotten” the instructions it was given at the outset.

Common mistake — thinking a large window settles everything

A 200,000-token window lets you send an entire book. That does not mean the model will make good use of it: the quality of attention degrades over very long texts, especially in the middle.

Sending the three relevant pages almost always gives better results — and costs a hundred times less.

💡 The model does not remember: everything is read back to it, every time, and you pay for that re-reading.

📚 Giving the model your documents

A model does not know your internal documents, your procedures, your prices. The method that solves this is called RAG — retrieval-augmented generation. The principle fits in one sentence: find the relevant passages first, then hand them to the model along with the question.

How it works, without the jargon

Documents are cut into chunks of a few hundred tokens. Each chunk is turned into a list of numbers representing its meaning — an embedding. The user's question goes through the same treatment, then you look for the chunks whose numbers are closest.

Those chunks are inserted into the prompt. The model answers on the strength of them, not on its training memory.

What RAG changes

Without RAG: “What is our cooling-off period?” → the model answers with general law, or invents.

With RAG: the question first goes and fetches clause 7 of your terms and conditions, then the model answers quoting it.

The difference is not the model's intelligence. It is that it has the right document in front of it.

Chunking is the real difficulty

Chunks that are too small lose their context: “this period is fourteen days” does not say which period. Too large, they dilute the relevant information in noise and cost more.

Most disappointing RAG systems do not suffer from the model, but from chunking done without thinking about the structure of the documents.

Common mistake — thinking RAG removes invention

If the search brings back no relevant chunk, the model answers anyway — with what it has. RAG reduces invention, it does not eliminate it.

The defence is to instruct the model explicitly to answer “I cannot find that information in the documents supplied”, and to show the sources used so the user can check.

💡 RAG does not make the model more knowledgeable. It saves it from having to guess.

🔧 Tools, or how an agent acts

A model on its own only produces text. What turns an assistant into an agent is the ability to call tools: query a database, send a message, create a file.

The actual mechanism

You describe the available tools to the model: their name, what they do, the parameters expected. When it judges a tool useful, it does not run it itself — it produces a call request, in structured form. Your program receives that request, decides whether to run it, and sends the result back.

This distinction is fundamental: the model proposes, your code disposes. That is where the whole security of an agent sits.

How an exchange with a tool unfolds

1. The user asks: “How many orders are pending?”

2. The model returns a call request: count_orders(status: "pending").

3. Your program checks the call is legitimate, runs it, gets 47.

4. The result goes back to the model, which writes the answer.

Four steps, only one of them done by the model: the middle one belongs entirely to you.

The principle of least privilege

An agent can only do what its tools allow. Giving it a “run any SQL query” tool amounts to handing the keys to the database to a system that can be manipulated by the text it reads.

Narrow, named tools — count_orders, read_invoice — rather than a general one: that is the difference between a useful agent and an incident.

Common mistake — the irreversible action with no confirmation

An agent that can delete, send or pay without human approval will eventually do so wrongly. Not necessarily out of malice: an ambiguous instruction is enough.

Separate the tools that read from the ones that write. The latter go through a confirmation.

💡 The model does nothing on its own. What it can break is exactly what you have given it.

⚖️ Choosing a model

The reflex is to take the best-performing model. That is rarely the right choice: on a simple, repeated task, a smaller model answers just as well, ten times faster and for a fraction of the price.

Four criteria, in this order

Quality on your task — not quality in general. A model that excels at mathematical reasoning is not necessarily better at sorting emails.

Latency. Two seconds against ten is not a detail in an interface where somebody is waiting.

Cost, billed per million tokens, separating input from output — output is generally several times more expensive.

Where the data runs. An open model hosted by you sends no data out; a remote service does. That question sometimes comes before all the others.

Worked example

A service handles 10,000 requests a month, each with 2,000 input tokens and 500 output.

Monthly volume: 20 million input tokens, 5 million output.

A price gap that looks trivial on one request becomes, at that volume, the difference between a viable service and one that gets shut down.

Common mistake — judging a model on leaderboards

Public leaderboards measure general capabilities on standardised test sets. They say nothing about your particular case, and models end up being optimised for those tests.

Put together twenty examples representative of your real task and run them past the candidates. Half a day's work, and an answer that actually concerns you.

💡 The right model is the smallest one that succeeds at your task — not the largest available.

📚 Going further

  • The vendors' own documentation: the only reliable sources for context sizes and prices, which change several times a year.
  • Online token counters: pasting a text and seeing its real weight avoids a lot of billing surprises.
  • Your national data protection authority: the obligations that apply as soon as processing involves personal data.
  • The European AI Act: the risk categories and the obligations attached to them, useful before deploying a system to the public.

One useful habit: before choosing a model, write your twenty test cases. They will then serve at every version change — and there will be some.
