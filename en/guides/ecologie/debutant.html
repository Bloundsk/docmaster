⚡ Key points to remember

  • Most of a device's impact is already paid for the first time you switch it on.

Introduction

This subject has an awkward peculiarity: the figures in circulation are often wrong, sometimes by a factor of ten, and they get repeated in good faith for years. “One email equals ten grams of CO₂”, “one hour of video equals 3.2 kilos”: both claims went round the media before being corrected.

This path therefore takes the problem the other way round: it starts by establishing where the impact is, which is solidly documented, before talking about habits. And it only calculates what can be calculated.

Warning — the figures assume French electricity

The calculations use the carbon intensity of French electricity, which is unusually low. The same operations elsewhere in Europe would give five to ten times more on everything to do with electricity consumption. The conclusions about manufacturing, which is the point of this level, are unaffected.

This guide is educational. The simulators' default values are orders of magnitude, editable: the manufacturing footprint of a device varies strongly by model and by calculation method.

Where the impact really is

🏭 The impact is in the manufacturing

This is the central fact of the field, and the one that overturns most intuitions: for a phone, a computer or a tablet, manufacturing accounts for most of the footprint. The electricity used over years of service weighs far less.

Why

Extracting and refining metals, producing components that need clean rooms, assembling, transporting: the chain is long, very energy-hungry, and located in countries whose electricity is far more carbon-intensive than France's.

Against that, a phone consumes a few kilowatt-hours a year. The ratio between the two is overwhelming, and it runs one way only.

Worked example — a phone kept 2½ years

Manufacturing: 60 kg CO₂e. Electricity: 5 kg CO₂e per year.

Manufacturing per year: 24 kg. Total: 29 kg per year, of which 83% is manufacturing alone.

The same device kept two years longer: 18.3 kg per year, that is 37% less. Without changing anything about how it is used.

The consequence, for every habit

If the impact is in the hardware, then advice about usage — sorting your email, lowering the resolution, closing tabs — is going after the small share.

It is not that such advice is wrong. It is that it occupies attention and gives the feeling of having acted, while the decision that counts — buy or keep — is taken elsewhere.

Common mistake — replacing in order to consume less

“This new model uses 30% less” is often true and rarely relevant: the 30% applies to the small share, and manufacturing the new device is immediate and complete.

Replacing a working device with a more frugal model almost always loses, and often by a lot.

💡 Most of the impact is already paid for the first time you switch the device on. The only variable left is how long.

⏳ Extending the lifetime

Since manufacturing dominates, everything comes down to one question: how many years will this device serve? Every year gained dilutes a footprint already paid for.

What shortens a device's life

  • Breakage. It is the leading cause of phone replacement, and the least fatal: a case and a screen protector cost a few pounds.
  • The battery. It ages before the rest, and replacing it costs a fraction of a new device.
  • The end of updates. A device that no longer receives security patches becomes hard to keep, whatever its physical condition.
  • Software getting heavier. Apps and sites become more demanding; a perfectly working device becomes uncomfortable.
What early replacement costs

A phone kept 2½ years comes to 29 kg CO₂e per year. The same kept 4½ years: 18.3 kg. The 37% gap is obtained without changing anything about the usage.

Conversely, replacing after six months takes the total to 125 kg per year, of which 96% is manufacturing alone. The same device, the same way of using it, four times the impact.

It is the duration, and it alone, that moves the result by a factor of seven.

Common mistake — keeping the old one “just in case”

A device sitting in a drawer has exactly the same manufacturing footprint as one in use. It has already been spent.

Selling or giving it away avoids manufacturing the one somebody would have bought instead. It is the one act that turns a useless object into an avoided impact.

💡 Every year gained on the lifetime does more than all the usage habits put together. That is not a figure of speech, it is the ratio of the numbers.

📧 The symbolic habits

A large share of the advice circulating on this subject concerns habits whose effect is real but tiny. Telling them apart is not cynicism: it is what lets you put the effort where it produces something.

The case of email

The figure of “ten grams of CO₂ per email” circulated for over ten years. It came from an old estimate that included the amortisation of hardware, and it was repeated without being rechecked.

Serious estimates put an ordinary email at a fraction of a gram. Deleting a thousand old messages therefore saves the equivalent of a few seconds of driving — once.

What weighs, and what only looks like it

Substantial effect: keeping your device longer, buying refurbished, repairing instead of replacing.

Real but very small effect: deleting your emails, unsubscribing from newsletters, lowering video quality.

No effect: clearing your browser cache — which forces a re-download of what was already there.

All three habits on the first line concern the hardware. None on the other two touches it.

Why this advice circulates so much

It is easy to apply, free, immediate and gratifying. It calls nothing into question — least of all a planned purchase. Advice saying “keep your phone two years longer” is less comfortable to pass on.

That is no reason to do nothing: it is a reason to check, before adopting a habit, whether it concerns the hardware or the usage.

Common mistake — assuming attention is unlimited

The problem with an ineffective habit is not that it is ineffective: it is that it consumes the available attention and goodwill, which are limited.

Somebody who sorts their email every month and changes phone every two years has made the wrong trade, without knowing it.

💡 Before adopting a habit, one question: does it concern the hardware or the usage? The first counts, the second barely.

🛒 Buying well

Since manufacturing decides, the purchase is where almost everything is settled. It is also the only moment when comparable information between models is available.

Refurbished

This is the most powerful lever available to an individual: a refurbished device avoids nearly all of a manufacturing cycle. No usage habit comes close to that order of magnitude.

The points to check are commercial rather than environmental: warranty, battery condition, provenance, right to return.

The criteria that count when buying new

  1. How long spare parts stay available, which governs real repairability.
  2. The number of years of guaranteed updates. That is often what determines end of life, before any breakdown.
  3. The official repairability or durability ratings, displayed at the point of sale where they exist.
  4. The size and power actually needed. An oversized device costs more to manufacture, without serving any better.
Common mistake — buying “green” rather than not buying

No new device has a zero footprint, and the gap between two models stays small next to the gap between buying and not buying.

The useful hierarchy is three words, in this order: keep, repair, refurbish. Buying new comes after, when the first three are exhausted.

💡 The most effective act in this guide is taken in a shop, and it often consists of buying nothing.

📚 Going further

  • Your national environment agency — studies on the environmental footprint of digital technology in your country, and practical sheets. The country matters: the electricity mix changes every figure.
  • Your telecoms regulator — several publish, with the environment agency, the reference work on how the impact is split.
  • Repairability and durability ratings — displayed at the point of sale in a growing number of countries.
  • Public repair and reuse directories — for finding a repairer, a donation point or a second-hand outlet near you.

One useful habit: count the electronic devices in your home, including the ones in a drawer. The total is surprising, and it is the only measure that genuinely counts.
