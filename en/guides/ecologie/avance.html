⚡ Key points to remember

  • The worst effect of a heavy service is not what it consumes: it is making a working device obsolete.
  • Two extra years on a hundred machines weigh more than every instruction sent to staff.
  • An uncollected electronic device is lost material as much as it is pollution.
  • A gain announced as a percentage says nothing until you know what the total did.
  • Efficiency per unit has improved for twenty years, and the total has gone up anyway.

Introduction

The first two levels addressed a user. This one addresses those who decide: designing a service, managing a fleet, weighing an investment.

That is where the levers change scale — and where faulty reasoning costs the most, because it is applied to thousands of devices.

Warning — the figures assume French electricity

The calculations use the carbon intensity of French electricity, which is unusually low. Elsewhere the electrical items would be five to ten times heavier; the manufacturing figures, which dominate this level, are unaffected.

This guide is educational. The default values are editable orders of magnitude.

The regulatory obligations described are the European framework, and they change: check their current state with the official sources for your country before relying on them.

Designing and deciding

🪶 Frugal design

2 min Designing a digital service frugally is generally presented as a matter of kilobytes saved.

Designing a digital service frugally is generally presented as a matter of kilobytes saved. That is the visible part, and the least important.

The real issue: not pushing people to replace

A service that demands a recent device makes working hardware obsolete. Since manufacturing dominates the impact, a replacement you caused costs more than years of data transfer.

The design question is therefore not “how much does my page weigh” but “at what device age does it become unusable”. The two are linked, but the second is the one that counts.

Worked example — a site at 3 MB per page

10,000 visits a month, at 0.06 kWh per gigabyte transferred.

Transferred per year: 351.6 GB. Estimated emissions: 1.3 kg CO₂e per year. Halving the weight: 0.6 kg.

In other words: halving an entire site saves less than a tenth of one manufactured phone. That does not make lightening pointless — but its justification lies elsewhere: speed, accessibility, and above all compatibility with old devices.

What makes a service frugal

  • It works on a five-year-old device and on a slow connection.
  • It starts nothing on its own — videos, animations, constant refreshes.
  • It does not try to stretch time spent. Infinite scroll, notifications, nudges: these mechanisms increase usage without giving the user anything.
  • It does what is expected of it, with no features added out of habit.

These criteria overlap almost entirely with those of accessibility and performance. That is good news: they can be argued in a meeting without invoking the environment at all.

Common mistake — green hosting as the answer

Announcing “decarbonised” hosting addresses the smallest of the three items, and lets everything else stay as it is.

It is not useless. It is simply not enough to call a service frugal, and it is often all that gets done.

💡 The worst effect of a heavy service is not what it consumes: it is making a device that still worked unusable.

🏢 The company fleet

1 min In an organisation the same ranking applies: the device fleet dominates everything else.

In an organisation the same ranking applies: the device fleet dominates everything else. And it is a far simpler lever to pull than a change of behaviour.

The decision that weighs

The replacement cycle for workstations is a single decision, taken by a few people, that applies to the whole fleet. No awareness campaign has that effort-to-result ratio.

Worked example — 100 machines, cycle from 3 to 5 years

Manufacturing of one machine: 200 kg CO₂e.

Today, at 3 years: 6,667 kg CO₂e per year.

At 5 years: 4,000 kg per year. Saving: 2,667 kg CO₂e every year, the equivalent of about 22,200 km by car.

One decision, once, asking nothing of anybody — and an effect renewed every year.

What makes extending possible

  1. Buying repairable hardware, with available parts and a replaceable battery.
  2. Choosing software that does not force replacement. That is often the real trigger, not a breakdown.
  3. Planning internal refurbishment: a machine handed back by a demanding user serves another role perfectly well.
  4. Taking the fleet out of the automatic accounting cycle. Depreciation over three years is not an obligation to replace after three years.
Common mistake — the awareness campaign instead of the decision

Asking staff to sort their email is visible, cheap, and has no measurable effect. Extending the fleet's replacement cycle is invisible and decisive.

When an organisation does the first without the second, the question is no longer environmental: it is one of communication.

💡 One decision about the fleet weighs more than ten thousand individual habits — and it asks nothing of anybody.

📜 The regulatory framework

2 min In a few years the subject has gone from awareness-raising to rules.

In a few years the subject has gone from awareness-raising to rules. Knowing the framework lets you lean on obligations rather than on convictions.

What follows describes the European framework; several of these mechanisms exist in similar form elsewhere, and some are specific to one country. Check what applies where you are.

The main mechanisms

MechanismWhat it does
Durability ratingDisplayed at the point of sale, it scores repairability and reliability. In France it succeeded the repairability index on several categories.
Spare parts availabilityThe manufacturer must state how long spare parts remain available.
WEEE schemeTake-back and treatment of waste electrical and electronic equipment, funded by a contribution included in the price.
Take-back on purchaseA retailer must take back the old device when selling an equivalent — often without mentioning it.
Planned obsolescenceA criminal offence in French law: deliberately shortening a product's life to increase replacement. Other countries treat it under unfair-practice rules, or not at all.
The weak link: collection

A substantial share of end-of-life devices never enters a treatment scheme. They stay in drawers, go out with ordinary rubbish, or are exported.

Yet those devices contain metals whose extraction is the heaviest part of manufacturing. Not recovering them loses twice: the pollution of the waste, and the extraction to be done again.

The useful act is mundane — a recycling centre, a shop, a collection point — and it is the one most often missing.

Common mistake — mistaking recycling for the solution

Recycling recovers some of the materials, never all, and consumes energy itself. It comes last in the hierarchy, after reduce, reuse and repair.

A message that puts recycling first moves attention from the purchase to the end of life — that is, from where everything is decided to where almost nothing is left to save.

💡 Recycling is the last step of the hierarchy, not the first. It starts at the purchase.

🔄 The rebound effect

This is the mechanism that explains why twenty years of technical progress have not brought digital's footprint down: efficiency per unit improves, and the number of units rises more.

The mechanism

An efficiency gain reduces the cost of a use. A cheaper use gets used more. The unit gain is real, and the total goes up anyway.

The examples are everywhere: storage becomes cheap, so everything gets kept; networks speed up, so content gets heavier; devices consume less, so there are more of them per household.

The question that settles it

A gain is almost always announced as a percentage and per unit: “40% more efficient”, “half the consumption per request”.

The only question that counts lies elsewhere: has the total gone down?

It is rarely asked, because it is hard to measure and the answer is often no. A model twice as efficient used ten times as often consumes five times more.

What this does not imply

The rebound effect does not say efficiency is useless: without it, the growth in usage would cost far more. It says efficiency is not enough, and that an efficiency gain announced on its own is not proof of a reduction.

The practical consequence is modest and demanding: measure totals, not ratios. A dashboard that tracks only intensities will improve indefinitely while absolute consumption rises.

Common mistake — treating “green digital” as an endpoint

A service can be efficient per request, hosted in a decarbonised data centre, and grow the total footprint — because it multiplied usage or accelerated hardware replacement.

None of those qualities is false. They simply answer a different question from the one being asked.

💡 A percentage improvement says nothing until you know the total. That is the question people avoid, and the only one that counts.

📚 Going further

  • Public frugal-design reference frameworks for digital services — criterion by criterion. Several countries now publish one.
  • Your national environment agency — practical guides on frugal design and responsible IT for organisations.
  • Public repair, donation and collection directories — for taking devices out of drawers and back into use.
  • National frameworks for reducing digital's environmental footprint — and the obligations they create for public bodies.

One useful habit: faced with any announced environmental improvement, look for the total rather than the percentage. If it is not given, that generally means it is not moving the right way.
