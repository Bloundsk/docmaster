⏱️ Productivity — Beginner

The four methods that have proved themselves. No prior knowledge needed.

🕒 Last updated: 16 August 2026

⚡ Key points

  • The Pomodoro technique alternates 25 minutes of focused work and 5 minutes of break.
  • GTD means capturing every task in a reliable system outside your head.
  • The Eisenhower matrix separates urgency from importance.
  • Reserve time every week for tasks that are important but not urgent.
  • The best tool is the one you actually use day to day.

Introduction

Productivity is a field saturated with advice, and that is precisely its problem: people spend more time choosing a method than doing the work. Installing a new tool also produces an immediate satisfaction very close to that of having made progress — without having made any.

The methods here have one thing in common: they do not try to make you work faster, but to reduce what costs you without producing anything — interruptions, repeated decisions, the mental load of what you are afraid of forgetting.

A useful warning: adopt only one at a time, and give it three weeks. Stacking four systems amounts to having none.

The basics

🍅 The Pomodoro technique

Work 25 minutes with no interruption at all, then take a 5-minute break. After four cycles, a long break of 15 to 30 minutes. That is all.

Why it works

Two mechanisms, both independent of willpower:

  • A short commitment is easy to make. "Work on this file" is vague and daunting; "give it 25 minutes" is doable. The method acts first against procrastination, not against tiredness.
  • The timer protects against interruptions. Picking up the thread after a break takes several minutes of restarting. Work interrupted six times an hour is not work that advances slowly: it is work that never starts.
The real cost of an interruption

A demanding task requiring 2 hours of concentration.

Unprotected: a notification roughly every 10 minutes. Each restart costs several minutes of getting back into context. Over two hours, a considerable share of the time goes into restarting — and quality suffers more than duration does.

In Pomodoro: four genuinely continuous 25-minute blocks. The work is finished before the second hour, and the result is better.

The gain does not come from greater speed. It comes from removing the time lost starting again.

In practice

  1. Write down one single task before starting the timer. A vague ambition produces a vague pomodoro.
  2. Turn off notifications. The method makes no sense without this step.
  3. If an idea comes up, note it on paper and carry on. Do not act on it.
  4. Actually take the break, and change the type of activity: stand up, drink, look into the distance. Scrolling a social network is not a break for attention.
Common mistake — skipping breaks when it is going well

It is tempting, and it is what produces exhaustion by the end of the day. Breaks are not a reward: they are part of the mechanism, and they are what lets the fourth block be as good as the first.

One more adjustment: there is nothing sacred about 25 minutes. Some creative tasks need 50 minutes just to get into. Adapt the length, keep the principle.

💡 The gain comes not from speed but from continuity. Twenty-five uninterrupted minutes beat a chopped-up hour.

✅ The GTD method

GTD (Getting Things Done) starts from an observation: memory is excellent for thinking and catastrophic for storing. As long as a task is written down nowhere, the mind keeps coming back to it — not to deal with it, only to check it has not been forgotten. It is a permanent, invisible tiredness.

The five steps

  1. Capture — get everything out of your head into one place: tasks, ideas, commitments, including the most trivial.
  2. Clarify — for each item: is it actionable? If not, bin it, file it or put it on hold. If yes, what is the next concrete physical action?
  3. Organise — sort by context: on the phone, at the computer, out shopping, to discuss with X.
  4. Review — a weekly review, without which the system goes stale and loses all credibility.
  5. Engage — choose according to context, time available and energy of the moment.

The central point: the next action

Rephrasing to unblock

"Organise the seminar" — a task pushed back for weeks. It is not an action, it is a project: the brain does not know where to start, so it avoids.

"Call the caterer on 01 23 45 67 89 to ask for a quote for 30 people" — a precise action, doable in four minutes, which gets done without thinking.

A task you have been putting off for a long time is almost always badly phrased. Rephrasing it is often enough to unblock it.

The two-minute rule

If an action takes less than two minutes, do it immediately. Noting it, filing it, rereading it and finding it again would cost more than doing it.

The weekly review

It is the step everyone abandons first, and the one that holds the whole thing together. Thirty minutes, the same day each week: empty the inboxes, reread the current projects, check that each has a defined next action.

Without it, the list fills with stale items, you stop believing in it, and you go back to memory — and therefore to the mental load.

Common mistake — building the perfect system

You can spend weeks designing an elegant architecture of tags, databases and automations. It is pleasant, it looks like work, and it is not.

A sheet of paper and a box kept seriously beat a sophisticated system abandoned after ten days.

💡 The point is not to do everything, but to have nothing left to remember. An unloaded mind thinks better.

🎯 The Eisenhower matrix

Two questions, four boxes. The tool looks simplistic, and that is exactly why it works when a list of thirty tasks paralyses you.

The quadrant that changes everything

Quadrant 2 — important but not urgent — is the only one with a lasting effect. Training, maintaining relationships, preventing problems, sleeping: none of it has a deadline, so none of it ever forces itself on you.

The mechanism is mechanical: the more you neglect quadrant 2, the more quadrant 1 fills up. A skill left unmaintained becomes an emergency retraining; a service postponed becomes a breakdown. Today's emergencies are the quadrant 2 you neglected six months ago.

The urgency test

Faced with a task that seems urgent, ask: "urgent for whom?"

Many emergencies are someone else's emergency, transferred by an insistent message. That does not mean it should be refused — but you should know you are doing it as a favour, not because it is one of your priorities.

Without that distinction, a whole day can fill up with other people's priorities.

Putting it into practice

Block a recurring slot in your calendar for quadrant 2, treated as an appointment that cannot be moved. What is not in the calendar does not exist: it will always be absorbed by an emergency.

Common mistake — classing everything as "important"

If all four boxes are equally full, the exercise achieved nothing. The matrix forces a trade-off, and a trade-off means some things will not be done.

Quadrant 4 must contain something. If it is empty, you have given nothing up.

💡 The urgent forces itself on you, the important never does. It is for the important that time must be reserved in advance.

🛠️ Task management tools

A tool makes nobody organised. It makes visible an organisation that already exists — or it makes visible its absence.

Choosing according to how you work

ToolWorks well forLimit
TodoistTask lists, deadlines, contexts. Close to GTD.Poorly suited to following a project visually
TrelloVisual tracking in columns (to do / doing / done)Becomes unreadable past a few dozen cards
NotionNotes and tasks together, highly customisableIts flexibility is a trap: you tinker instead of doing
PaperDaily use, with no notifications and no distractionsNo search, no reminders, no backup

The criteria that really count

  • Speed of capture. If noting a task takes more than five seconds, you will stop doing it — and the system collapses at that precise moment.
  • One place only. Three apps in parallel guarantee that none is up to date. An imperfect but single system beats three perfect ones.
  • Daily consultation. A list you do not open is a dead list.
  • The ability to get out. Check that your data can be exported: tools shut down or change their pricing.
The three-week test

Every new tool seems wonderful in the first few days — it is the novelty that pleases, not the tool.

The real question comes in the third week, when the workload returns and the enthusiasm has gone: do you still open it?

If yes, keep it and stop looking. If not, the problem is probably not the tool.

Common mistake — organised procrastination

Reorganising your lists, comparing apps, watching method videos: it looks like work and it is not. It is even a particularly comfortable form of procrastination, since it comes with a clear conscience.

Warning sign: if you have changed tool more than twice this year, the subject is not the tool.

💡 The best tool is the one you will still open in three weeks. Every other criterion comes a long way behind.

📚 Going further

  • “Getting Things Done”, David Allen — the founding work of GTD. Dense; the first three chapters are enough to start.
  • “Deep Work”, Cal Newport — on sustained concentration and why it is becoming rare. A good complement to Pomodoro.
  • “Atomic Habits”, James Clear — the approach through habits rather than motivation. The most directly applicable of the three.
  • Blocking apps (Cold Turkey, Freedom, built-in focus modes) — more effective than willpower, because they act before the temptation.
  • Your phone's timer — free and sufficient for Pomodoro. No dedicated app is needed.

A method tip: do not read these three books one after another. Take one, apply a single idea for a month, and only open the next if the need arises. Reading about productivity is itself one of the most common ways of avoiding it.
