Making yourself understood. No prior knowledge needed.

⚡ Key points

  • The most neglected question is not "what do I want?" but "what do I do if I don't get it?".
  • Restating what you heard very often reveals that you had understood something else.
  • The conclusion goes at the start, not the end: your reader may not get that far.
  • A 400-word message sent to eight people takes up 13 minutes of work.
  • "93% of communication is non-verbal" is a distortion of a study that did not say that.

Introduction

This field is saturated with advice, much of it resting on claims that were never checked. This path sticks to what can be shown — and it starts with the least spectacular part: preparing, listening, and structuring.

The intermediate level will cover negotiation proper, with the tools that make it calculable. This one lays down what they are useless without.

Warning

This guide is educational. The techniques described come from published work, but how well they work depends heavily on context, on people and on culture — these are not recipes.

The most widespread claims in the field are set aside when they do not hold up. That is sometimes counter-intuitive, and it is deliberate.

Making yourself understood

🎯 Preparing before you speak

2 min Most of an important conversation is decided before it starts.

Most of an important conversation is decided before it starts. Not by rehearsing sentences, but by answering four questions people almost never ask themselves.

The four questions

  1. What do I want to get? In one sentence, and in a checkable form. "Being better recognised" cannot be negotiated; "a 5% rise from 1 January" can be discussed.
  2. What am I ready to give up? A demand with no identified trade-off leaves the other side able only to refuse everything or grant everything.
  3. What does the other side want? If you do not know, plan to ask them — it is a question to be asked out loud.
  4. What do I do if we get nowhere? This is the decisive question, and the one people skip. It determines your real position, not the one you display.
The weak point in your own case

Useful preparation includes what works against you. The other side will find it anyway: better to have seen it coming than to discover it in the room.

Two options then exist, and both beat silence: state it yourself, which defuses its effect, or prepare the answer, which avoids improvising at the worst moment.

What does not work is hoping it goes unnoticed.

Choosing the moment and the channel

An important request slipped in at the end of a meeting, or into a message among thirty, starts with a handicap that has nothing to do with its content.

The useful habit fits in one sentence: do not let the setting be imposed on you. Ask for a dedicated slot, choose writing or speech according to what serves the point, and avoid handling in writing what needs a discussion.

Common mistake — preparing your arguments and nothing else

Classic preparation consists of listing what you are going to say. It is the least useful part: it assumes the exchange is about persuading by accumulation.

What changes the result is knowing what you do if it fails — because that alone determines how far you can go.

💡 It is not your best argument that sets your position, it is what you get if the discussion fails.

👂 Really listening

2 min Listening is presented as a moral quality.

Listening is presented as a moral quality. It is first of all a collection technique: most of the information you need is said, and is not heard because you are preparing your answer.

Restating, and what it reveals

Restating fits in one formula: "so if I understand correctly, …" followed by what you understood, then a silence.

Its point is not to make a good impression. It is that in a surprising proportion of cases, the other person corrects you — and you discover you have been discussing two different things for ten minutes.

Silence, the most uncomfortable tool

After an answer, leaving three seconds of silence very often produces a second answer — more precise, more honest, sometimes decisive.

That silence is unpleasant to hold: the reflex is to fill it. That is why it works, and why it is so little used.

No technique in this guide takes less effort and pays as much.

Questions that open

A closed question — "do you agree?" — invites one word. An open question — "what bothers you about that?" — invites an explanation, and the explanation is what is useful.

The most profitable question in the field is five words long: "why does that matter to you?". It moves from what the other side is asking for to the reason they are asking — and that is where the solutions are.

Common mistake — listening in order to reply

Preparing your comeback while the other person talks is almost universal, and it costs you most of what is said.

The remedy is mechanical rather than moral: take notes of what they say. A hand busy transcribing cannot draft the reply at the same time.

💡 Restating costs ten seconds. Discovering after an hour that you were talking about something else costs the hour.

🗣️ Structuring a message

2 min The natural structure of a presentation — context, analysis, then conclusion — is the structure of discovery.

The natural structure of a presentation — context, analysis, then conclusion — is the structure of discovery. It is not the one that is easiest to follow, because it assumes the listener stays to the end.

Conclusion first

The rule is simple and counter-intuitive: start with what you want remembered, then develop it. "I propose delaying the launch by a month, for three reasons" can be followed effortlessly. The same three reasons followed by the proposal require holding everything in memory.

This structure has a second effect: it forces you to know what you want to say. Many presentations that end on a vague conclusion simply did not have one.

"93% of communication is non-verbal" — what the study actually says

The figure comes from Albert Mehrabian's work, and is quoted everywhere as if it described communication in general. It describes nothing of the sort.

The experiments used isolated words, spoken with a tone and expression that contradicted their meaning, and measured the perception of an attitude — not the transmission of information.

In other words: when the tone contradicts the words, people believe the tone. That is an interesting and far narrower result. Mehrabian himself repeatedly said the generalisation was wrong.

The practical consequence is the opposite of the one usually drawn: content matters, and it matters a great deal.

One idea per sentence

Long sentences do not make a point richer: they force the listener to hold several elements in memory while receiving others.

The useful marker: if a sentence cannot be said in one breath, it probably contains two ideas. Separating them does not simplify the substance, it makes the substance reachable.

Common mistake — polishing the form at the expense of the request

Many messages are well turned, pleasant to read, and obtain nothing: the request has been diluted out of politeness, or pushed to the end.

Politeness fits in one formula; the request must be prominent. The two do not conflict — but when in doubt, the request wins.

💡 Put the conclusion at the start. If you do not know what it is, that is not a problem of form.

📧 Writing to be read

2 min A work message is not read, it is triaged.

A work message is not read, it is triaged. It passes first through a subject line, then through the first two lines. What comes after only exists if those two stages have been cleared.

The subject line, and the first two lines

A good subject says what it is about and what is expected: "2027 budget — your approval before Friday" gets handled; "Budget update" gets postponed.

The first two lines carry the request. Context, history and explanations come afterwards — for those who need them.

Worked example — a 400-word message to 8 recipients

Reading time: 1.6 minutes per person. Total time taken up: 13 minutes.

The same message cut to 200 words: 6.4 minutes. Seven minutes recovered, once — and on every message.

Along the way: beyond about 150 words, most readers skim instead of reading. A 400-word message is therefore not read twice as much as a 200-word one: it is read less well.

One request, one recipient

A message with six recipients in "To" and three different requests generally obtains nothing: each assumes somebody else is dealing with it.

The rule that settles most cases: one person alone in "To", the others in copy, and one request — or a numbered list with a name against each line.

Common mistake — writing what should have been said

A disagreement, a criticism, a delicate negotiation: these subjects write badly. Tone is lost, rereadings pile up, and an exchange that would have taken five minutes takes three days.

The marker: if you are rereading your message a third time to adjust the tone, it should not have been written. Writing comes afterwards, to put things on record.

💡 The subject line and the first two lines decide whether the rest is read. That is where all the care should go.

📚 Going further

  • Work on active listening: restating and silence, two simple and robust techniques.
  • “The Pyramid Principle”, Barbara Minto: conclusion first, argument afterwards. The reference on structuring a work message.
  • Albert Mehrabian's papers — worth reading to see the gap between what they establish and what they are made to say.
  • “Nonviolent Communication”, Marshall Rosenberg: the distinction between observable fact and interpretation, taken up at the advanced level.

A useful habit: in your next important exchange, restate once — "so if I understand correctly, …" — and stay quiet for three seconds. It is the most profitable move in this level.
