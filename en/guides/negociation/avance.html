Difficult conversations, and knowing when to stop.

⚡ Key points

  • It is not the criticism that blocks a conversation, it is the intention attributed.
  • A fact can be discussed; a presumed intention cannot — it can only be denied.
  • Time already invested should change nothing about the decision — and it almost always changes everything.
  • The answer to a tactic is neither to suffer it nor to use one: it is to name it.
  • Many negotiations carry on past the point where they cost more than they bring in.

Introduction

The first two levels assumed a good-faith discussion between parties looking for a deal. This one is about what happens when that is not the case: head-on disagreement, your own blind spots, tactics, and the moment to stop.

Warning

This guide is educational. The tactics described are there to be recognised, not used: in a relationship meant to last, they cost more than they bring in.

Some situations are not negotiations at all. Harassment, discrimination and pressure at work call for specific legal remedies, which vary by country — find the ones that apply where you are.

When things get complicated

😤 Difficult conversations

Raising a criticism, breaking bad news, expressing a fundamental disagreement: these conversations almost always fail at the same point, and for the same reason.

Observable fact versus attributed intention

"You don't take this project seriously" is not a criticism that can be discussed: it is a presumed intention. The only possible response is to deny it, and the conversation stops there.

"The report was due on Monday, I received it on Thursday, and that is the third time this quarter" describes facts. They can be confirmed, explained, disputed — but they open something.

The rule fits in one sentence: describe what a camera would have recorded. Anything that could not have been filmed is interpretation.

The three-part structure

1. The fact. Dated, observable, with no adjectives.

2. The effect. What it concretely produces — "I had to move the meeting and redo the figures myself". Not "it's disrespectful".

3. The request. Precise and turned towards what comes next: "next time, tell me as soon as you see the deadline will not hold".

Without the third part, all that is left is a complaint. It is the one most often forgotten, and the only one that can change anything.

What makes a criticism acceptable

Two material conditions weigh as much as the wording: one to one, never in front of others; and with time allowed for the answer. A criticism slipped in at the end of a meeting, with no room to reply, will be experienced as a sentence.

Finally, separate what is negotiable from what is not. Presenting a decision already taken as an open discussion is the surest way to lose the other person's trust.

Common mistake — the compliment sandwich

The technique of framing a criticism between two compliments is widely taught and largely ineffective: the listener remembers one or the other, rarely both, and mostly learns to distrust compliments.

What works is simpler: say what is wrong, clearly, once, with a request — and say the positive things at other times, when they will be believed.

💡 Describe what a camera would have recorded. Everything else will be disputed, and rightly so.

🧠 Biases

The costliest errors in negotiation do not come from the other side: they come from regular, predictable mechanisms, which are far easier to see in other people than in yourself.

The four that cost most

  1. Sunk cost. Six months of discussions do not make a bad deal acceptable. The time spent is lost whatever happens; only the comparison with your alternative counts.
  2. Assumed zero sum. Believing that what one side gains, the other loses. True for a single variable, false as soon as there are several — and it is the default assumption.
  3. Commitment through consistency. Clinging to a stated number so as not to look changeable, even when new information makes it absurd.
  4. Overestimating what the other side knows. You assume your constraints are obvious when they are invisible from outside — and so you never explain them.
The only comparison worth making

A deal is judged against your alternative. Not against what you hoped for, nor against what the other side got, nor against the time you spent.

It is an unpleasant comparison: it takes away the feeling of having won, and it also takes away the feeling of having lost. Its merit is being the only one that corresponds to a reality.

A deal that feels disappointing can be excellent, and one wrung out of a hard fight can be worse than walking away.

What limits the damage

None of these biases disappears through being known. What works is procedural: write down your walk-away point before starting, and reread it at the moment of deciding.

A number written in the cold resists the pressure of the moment far better than an intention held in your head.

Common mistake — feeling safe because you know the bias

Knowing what anchoring is does not protect you from anchoring: the effect has been measured in professional negotiators, in judges, in experts warned about the set-up.

Knowledge is not enough; a written procedure is.

💡 Time already invested should change nothing about the decision. It almost always changes it.

🎭 Recognising manipulation

Some tactics come up so regularly that they have names. Knowing them is not for using them — in a relationship meant to last they cost far more than they bring in — but for not suffering them.

The most common

TacticWhat it looks like
Artificial urgency"The offer expires tonight" — to prevent comparison and thought
False concessionA tiny gesture presented as a major effort, to call for a real one in return
Last-minute nibbleAn extra demand once the deal is nearly done, when giving up would cost everything
Absent authority"My boss says no" — unverifiable, and moving the negotiation towards someone unreachable
Figure with no sourceA number stated as obvious, which it becomes rude to question
Personal attackQuestioning the person rather than the substance, to shift the discussion
The answer: name it, calmly

Neither suffer it nor hit back. Describing the tactic out loud, without aggression, is enough in most cases to stop it — because it only works while unnoticed.

"I notice the deadline is very short. Is it negotiable?" handles artificial urgency. "We agreed yesterday on this scope; this request changes it" handles the nibble.

Neither sentence accuses. They simply make visible what was meant to stay implicit.

The real defence

All these techniques assume you cannot walk away. Your alternative is the only general protection: with it, artificial urgency has no grip, the last-minute nibble can be refused, and the absent authority becomes irrelevant.

It is also why tactics are more frequent in asymmetric relationships: they work all the better when the other side has nowhere to go.

Common mistake — replying in kind

Answering with a symmetric tactic turns a negotiation into a confrontation, and destroys the possibility of asymmetric trades — the ones that produce the best deals.

It also damages the relationship, which costs dearly as soon as you have to meet again.

💡 A named tactic stops working. It only has an effect while it passes for natural.

🛑 Knowing when to stop

The least asked question in a negotiation is what it costs. A disagreement that drags on consumes time, attention and the relationship — and often exceeds the gap still to be bridged.

The calculation nobody does

It is elementary: time spent, multiplied by what it is worth, compared with what is still at stake. If it is never done, that is because it generally makes both sides wrong.

Worked example — €1,500 of gap remaining

Six predictable weeks of deadlock, three hours a week spent on the matter, at €45 an hour.

Time spent: 18 hours. Cost of that time: €810 — that is 54% of the remaining gap. The gap still justifies talking, but only just.

The same deadlock stretched over twenty weeks costs €2,700: 180% of the gap. At that point both parties have lost, whatever the final deal.

Walking away, and how to do it

Leaving the table is neither a failure nor a threat: it is exercising your alternative. It still has to be done properly, because files get reopened.

Three elements are enough: thank them, say what would have made a deal possible, and leave the door open. "We cannot go beyond X. If things change on your side, get back in touch."

This wording has a frequent side effect: it sometimes restarts the negotiation on better terms — because it makes walking away credible.

Common mistake — closing just to be done with it

The fatigue of a long negotiation pushes people to accept what they would have refused on day one. The feeling of relief is immediate, and says nothing about the quality of the deal.

This is exactly the situation that a walk-away point written in advance prevents. Reread it at the moment of signing, not before.

💡 Leaving the table is not a failure of the negotiation: it is using what gave you power from the start.

📚 Going further

  • “Difficult Conversations”, Stone, Patton & Heen — from the Harvard Negotiation Project: the distinction between facts, intentions and identity.
  • “Thinking, Fast and Slow”, Daniel Kahneman: the biases quoted here, and why knowing them is not enough to be protected from them.
  • “Influence”, Robert Cialdini: the mechanisms of influence, described so they can be recognised.
  • Workplace advisory bodies: for situations that call for a legal remedy rather than a negotiation. What exists varies by country.

A useful habit: write your walk-away point on a piece of paper before going in, and only reread it at the moment of deciding. It is the only protection that survives fatigue.
