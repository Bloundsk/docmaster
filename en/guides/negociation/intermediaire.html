🤝 Negotiation — Intermediate

What can be worked out before you sit down at the table.

⚡ Key points

  • Your power comes not from your arguments but from what you get if you walk away.

  • A position is what you ask for, an interest is why you ask for it.

Introduction

The beginner level was about making yourself understood. This one is about negotiation itself — and about the often ignored fact that it can largely be worked out before it starts.

Four ideas are enough. They come from the Harvard Negotiation Project's work and all translate into figures.

Warning

This guide is educational. The amounts in the examples are arbitrary and serve to show a mechanism.

The tools described assume a negotiation where both parties can walk away. In a very asymmetric relationship — employer and lone employee, landlord and tenant — they apply differently. That is the subject of the advanced level.

What can be worked out

🚪 Your alternative

This is the central idea of the whole path, and the least well known: your power in a negotiation comes not from your arguments, your confidence or your preparation. It comes from what you get if you walk away.

The BATNA

It is called the best alternative to a negotiated agreement — BATNA. It is your best option in the absence of a deal: another supplier, another job, keeping what you have, going to court.

It sets your walk-away point: no offer below it deserves accepting, since leaving is worth more. And it explains why two equally skilled people do not have the same power.

Worked example — an offer of €30,000

Your alternative is worth €28,000. The offer on the table is €30,000.

Gain compared with walking away: + €2,000. Carrying on the discussion would cost about €500 in time and energy.

Verdict: you have room to continue. If the offer were €28,200 it would still beat walking away — but continuing would cost more than it would bring in.

Improve it before negotiating

This is the most useful practical consequence: you strengthen your position before the negotiation, not during it. Getting a second proposal, checking an alternative, preparing a plan B — each of these moves the walk-away point.

And symmetrically: estimating the other side's alternative tells you how far they can go. A supplier who has only you as a customer does not negotiate like one with twenty.

Common mistake — bluffing with no alternative

"I can easily go elsewhere", said when it is not true, works once, and is paid for afterwards: if the other side calls the bluff, all that is left is to give in, from a weakened position.

A real alternative, however modest, beats a threat that is credible for five minutes.

💡 With no alternative worked out in figures, you cannot tell whether an offer is good — only whether it feels pleasant.

📏 The zone of agreement

Each side has a limit. Between the two lies — or does not — a space where agreement is possible. Knowing it changes entirely what is being discussed.

What the zone determines

If your maximum exceeds the other side's minimum, a zone of agreement exists. The negotiation is no longer about whether a deal is possible, but about how that zone is shared.

If it does not exist, no amount of insisting will produce a deal on price alone. That is valuable information, and it saves weeks.

Worked example — two limits

Your maximum: €32,000. Their estimated minimum: €27,000.

Zone of agreement: €27,000 to €32,000. Width: €5,000. Midpoint: €29,500.

What is at stake is therefore not the deal: it is the split of that €5,000. Everything said will serve to move the cursor within that range.

When there is no zone

This is the most instructive case. Only two responses exist, and only one is useful.

The useless one: insisting, repeating the arguments, dragging it out. The useful one: add a variable. Payment terms, volume, length of commitment, warranty, associated services, delivery schedule.

A negotiation with a single variable is a split: what one side gains, the other loses. As soon as there are two, trades become possible — and that is what unblocks most stuck situations.

Common mistake — announcing your limit

Saying "my maximum is 32,000" amounts to offering 32,000: nobody will go below it any more.

The limit is there to help you decide, not to be communicated. It is kept private, like the alternative it rests on.

💡 With no zone of agreement on price, the only way out is to add a variable. Insisting does not create one.

⚓ Anchoring

The first number stated influences everything that follows, even when everyone knows it is negotiable and believes themselves immune. It is one of the best replicated effects in the psychology of decision.

The arithmetic mechanism

No psychology is needed to see it: the famous "let's split the difference" gives a result that depends entirely on the two opening offers. Whoever states the first one sets half the equation.

Worked example — aiming for €29,000

You are aiming for €29,000. You expect an offer of €27,000 from the other side.

For the midpoint to land on your target, your opening offer must be €31,000 — that is €2,000 above what you are aiming for.

Midpoint obtained: €29,000, exactly your target. The arithmetic is trivial; what is not is thinking of it before stating a number.

Should you go first?

The traditional advice — "never state the first number" — is contradicted by the work on anchoring: whoever opens steers the discussion, provided their number stays plausible.

The exception is about information: if you do not know what the thing being negotiated is worth, opening exposes you to anchoring against yourself. Open when you know; let them open when you do not.

Common mistake — the implausible anchor

An opening offer far removed from the market does not move the discussion: it disqualifies it. The other side concludes that you do not know the subject, or that you are not negotiating in good faith.

An effective anchor is ambitious and defensible — it must be justifiable in one sentence.

💡 "Let's split the difference" sounds fair and depends entirely on the first number stated.

🧭 Positions and interests

A position is what you ask for. An interest is the reason you ask for it. Positions clash; interests, often, do not.

The distinction that unblocks things

Two people who want the same orange split the fruit in half. One wanted the juice, the other the peel for a cake: each could have had everything. Somebody just had to ask why.

The example is deliberately simplified. The mechanism is the most useful in the whole path: as long as you discuss positions, only a split exists. As soon as you know the interests, other solutions appear.

What the question "why" reveals

Position: "I need a 15% discount". Possible interest: staying within an annual budget, justifying the spend internally, or matching what a competitor was offered.

Depending on which, the solution differs: spreading payment over two financial years, providing an itemised quote, or adjusting the scope rather than the price.

None of those three appears while you are arguing about the percentage.

Look for the asymmetric trade

The best deal is not the one where each side gives up half: it is the one where each gets what costs them least to give and is worth most to receive.

Hence the question to prepare before every negotiation: what costs me little and is worth a lot to them? A deadline, a reference, an exclusivity, flexibility on the schedule. These things never appear in the opening discussion.

Common mistake — assuming everything is zero-sum

Believing that what one side gains the other loses is true for a single variable and false as soon as there are several. It is nonetheless almost everyone's default assumption.

It has a measurable cost: it stops you looking for asymmetric trades, and therefore from finding the deals nobody had considered.

💡 "Why does that matter to you?" is the most profitable question in the field, and the least asked.

📚 Going further

  • “Getting to Yes”, Fisher & Ury — the founding work of the Harvard Negotiation Project. The BATNA and the positions/interests distinction come from it.
  • Tversky & Kahneman on anchoring: one of the best replicated effects in the psychology of decision.
  • “Negotiation Genius”, Malhotra & Bazerman: the zone of agreement, asymmetric trades and biases, with many worked cases.
  • Mediators and conciliators — most countries have low-cost routes for when a negotiation fails; check what exists where you are.

A useful habit: before your next negotiation, write a single line — what you get if it fails. That line decides everything else.
