**First, make yourself understood.** Start with the conclusion. And treat the
claim that ninety-three percent of communication is non-verbal with caution:
the original study only tested isolated words spoken with a contradictory tone,
so what you say matters a great deal. Write like a newspaper: the important
request goes in the headline, not at the bottom.

**Second, prepare before you sit down.** Your power does not come from your
arguments but from what you will do if you walk away. A solid alternative is a
spare parachute: you negotiate calmly because you know you can land. Watch out
for anchoring, too — whoever names the first number decides the arithmetic when
someone suggests splitting the difference.

**Finally, when it becomes a disagreement.** Separate what you observed from
what you assume about intentions. Describe things as a security camera would
record them: actions and times, not motives. And know when to stop, instead of
carrying on because of the time already spent.

This path is education, not personal advice. Your greatest strength in any room
is not your best argument, but knowing exactly what you will do if you leave
with nothing.
