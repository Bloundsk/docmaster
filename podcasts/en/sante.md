**First, what you can control: your physical set-up.** Most of the damage comes
from positions held for a long time. A laptop flat on the desk guarantees a bad
posture — like holding shopping at arm's length: painless at first, hard on the
shoulders by the seventh hour. Sport at the weekend does not cancel out nine
hours a day sitting down, so break those hours up. And looking into the
distance through a window is a free way to rest your eyes.

**Second, what happens in your head.** Burnout is not a sign that you handle
stress badly; it is a failure of organisation — high demands combined with
little room to manoeuvre. Stress becomes chronic when the load never drops, and
real recovery means switching off completely, not lying on the sofa going over
your emails.

**Finally, when things go wrong.** The occupational doctor is bound by medical
confidentiality: their job is to prevent harm, not to report to your manager.
Employers have a legal duty of prevention. In serious situations such as
harassment, the first step is to write down the facts and date them straight
away.

This path is education, not personal legal or medical advice. Wellbeing at work
comes down to adjusting what you can control, claiming room to handle your
mental load, and knowing where to turn when the system fails.
